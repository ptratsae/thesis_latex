\chapter{Market congestion and two-phase balancing}
\label{chap:congestion_balancing}

Now that both the user-side recommender system
(Chapter~\ref{chap:user_recommendation}) and the recruiter-side recommender
system (Chapter~\ref{chap:recruiter_recommendation}) have been developed, the
next step is to mitigate the congestion that emerges as a consequence of
recommendation at scale. As introduced in Section~\ref{sec:prelim_twosided},
two distinct forms arise: job-side congestion, in which traffic concentrates on
a small subset of job postings, and candidate-side congestion, in which recruiter
attention concentrates on a small subset of candidates. Both forms are first
addressed in isolation using the Gini-based measurement framework and
market-maker intervention strategies defined in
Sections~\ref{sec:prelim_fairness} and~\ref{sec:prelim_zones}. The two
strategies are then combined into a two-phased approach where first job congestion
is addressed, followed by candidate-side congestion. Job-side congestion is measured directly from the application
distribution in the dataset; candidate-side congestion is measured by simulating
recruiter shortlisting using the recruiter-side model based on the actual applications. The empirical evidence
and full experimental evaluation of all strategies is deferred to
Chapter~\ref{chap:experiments}.

\section{Job-Side Market-Maker Intervention}
\label{sec:job_congestion}
% ============================================================

Job-side congestion arises when the recommendation distribution concentrates
applications on a small subset of popular job postings, leaving the majority
under-applied. To redistribute traffic more equitably, a market-maker
modification is applied to the job ranking score during the recommendation
phase. The modification is a function of each job's current application count
at the time a user is being processed, so that already-popular jobs are
progressively penalised and under-applied jobs receive a visibility boost.

Concretely, at the moment user $u$ is being processed, let $c_j^{(t)}$ denote
the number of applications job $j$ has already received from previously
processed users in the current simulation run. The modified ranking score for
the $(u, j)$ pair is:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{s}_{uj} = s_{uj} + \delta\!\left(c_j^{(t)}\right),
\end{equation}
where $s_{uj}$ is the base relevance score from the two-tower model and
$\delta(\cdot)$ is a congestion modification function. Jobs within the safe
zone $[\tau_{\min}^{\text{job}}, \tau_{\max}^{\text{job}}]$ receive no
modification ($\delta = 0$). Starved jobs ($c_j^{(t)} < \tau_{\min}^{\text{job}}$)
receive a positive boost to increase their visibility. Congested jobs
($c_j^{(t)} > \tau_{\max}^{\text{job}}$) receive a negative penalty to reduce
further applications. Jobs exceeding a hard cap $H^{\text{job}}$ are penalised heavily to ensure they are at the bottom of the ranking. Three functional forms for $\delta$ are considered,
each making a different assumption about how aggressively congestion should be
penalised.

\paragraph{Linear modification.}
The penalty grows at a constant rate per unit of congestion beyond the safe
zone:
\begin{equation}
    \delta_{\text{lin}}(c) = \begin{cases}
        \min\!\bigl(\gamma_b \cdot (\tau_{\min} - c),\; B\bigr) & c < \tau_{\min}, \\
        0 & \tau_{\min} \leq c \leq \tau_{\max}, \\
        \max\!\bigl(-\gamma_p \cdot (c - \tau_{\max}),\; -P\bigr) & c > \tau_{\max},
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}
where $B$ and $P$ are the maximum boost and penalty magnitudes respectively,
and $\gamma_b$, $\gamma_p$ are the rates per unit of deviation from the safe
zone.

\paragraph{Quadratic modification.}
The penalty grows quadratically, starting slowly for mildly congested jobs and
accelerating as congestion increases:
\begin{equation}
    \delta_{\text{quad}}(c) = \begin{cases}
        \min\!\bigl(\kappa_b \cdot (\tau_{\min} - c)^2,\; B\bigr) & c < \tau_{\min}, \\
        0 & \tau_{\min} \leq c \leq \tau_{\max}, \\
        \max\!\bigl(-\kappa_p \cdot (c - \tau_{\max})^2,\; -P\bigr) & c > \tau_{\max},
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}
where $\kappa_b$ and $\kappa_p$ are scaling parameters.

\paragraph{Sigmoid modification.}
A smooth S-curve naturally bounds the modification within $[-P, B]$ without
requiring explicit clamping:
\begin{equation}
    \delta_{\text{sig}}(c) = \begin{cases}
        B \cdot \!\left(\dfrac{2}{1 + e^{-k(\tau_{\min} - c)}} - 1\right)
            & c < \tau_{\min}, \\[6pt]
        0 & \tau_{\min} \leq c \leq \tau_{\max}, \\[6pt]
        -P \cdot \!\left(\dfrac{2}{1 + e^{-k(c - \tau_{\max})}} - 1\right)
            & c > \tau_{\max},
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}
where $k$ controls the steepness of the curve.

All three functions share the same boundary values ($B$, $P$,
$H^{\text{job}}$) but differ in how quickly the modification grows
between the safe zone and the hard cap. The linear form applies a
constant rate of change, reaching its maximum penalty only at the hard
cap. The quadratic form is lenient on moderately congested jobs but
accelerates once congestion becomes severe, concentrating the penalty
on the heaviest tail. The sigmoid form is the most aggressive early
on, applying a steep penalty as soon as a job crosses the congested
threshold $\tau_{\max}^{\text{job}}$, before levelling off toward the hard cap. The specific
parameter values used in the experiments are reported in
Section~\ref{sec:exp_congestion_setting} and the empirical results are presented in Section~\ref{sec:exp_job_congestion}.

\section{Candidate-side market-maker intervention}
\label{sec:user_congestion}
% ============================================================

User-side congestion arises when recruiter attention concentrates on a small
subset of candidates: strong applicants accumulate shortlisting events across
many job inboxes simultaneously, while weaker-signalled but potentially
qualified candidates are overlooked entirely. The same market-maker framework
developed for the job side is adapted here to act on the recruiter's shortlisting
decision rather than the user's application decision.

At the moment job $j$'s inbox is being processed, let $v_u^{(t)}$ denote the
number of times candidate $u$ has already been shortlisted in the current
simulation run. The modified recruiter ranking score is:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{r}_{uj} = r_{uj} + \delta_{\text{cand}}\!\left(v_u^{(t)}\right),
\end{equation}
where $r_{uj}$ is the recruiter model's base suitability probability and
$\delta_{\text{cand}}(\cdot)$ uses the same three functional forms as
Section~\ref{sec:job_congestion} but parameterised with candidate-side
thresholds $\tau_{\min}^{\text{cand}}$ and $\tau_{\max}^{\text{cand}}$. The
top-$k$ candidates by $\hat{r}_{uj}$ are shortlisted for each job. As more
job inboxes are processed sequentially, candidates who have been shortlisted
frequently accumulate negative modifications, naturally redirecting recruiter
attention toward candidates who have not yet been surfaced. The candidate-side
parameter values used in the experiments are reported in
Section~\ref{sec:exp_congestion_setting}. The empirical results are presented in Section~\ref{sec:exp_user_congestion}.

\section{Dual-sided hybrid strategy}
\label{sec:hybrid_strategy}
% ============================================================

The job-side and user-side mitigations described above address each form of
congestion in isolation. The job-side intervention modifies which jobs users
apply to; the user-side intervention modifies which candidates recruiters
shortlist. A more principled approach recognises that both problems share a
common root: the scoring function in each case considers only one side of the
market. The Two-Tower model reflects user preferences but is blind to whether
the candidate is actually competitive for the role. The recruiter model reflects
employer preferences but plays no role in shaping which users apply in the first
place.

The dual-sided hybrid strategy addresses this by running both models together as
a sequential two-phase pipeline. Crucially, because the system is two-sided,
there is no single recommendation list — each phase produces a different output
for a different audience:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Phase~1} answers the question \emph{``which jobs should user
    $u$ apply to?''} and produces a ranked job list per user. Based on this ranking, a simulated application pool is generated by taking the top-$k$ jobs for each user, where $k$ matches their application count in the dataset. 
    \item \textbf{Phase~2} answers the question \emph{``among all applicants to
    job $j$, who should be shortlisted?''} and produces a candidate shortlist
    per job.
\end{itemize}
The two phases use independent scoring formulas and independent running counters, because they operate in opposite directions on different questions. Phase~2 takes the application pool produced by Phase~1 as its input: Phase~1 decides who is in each job's inbox, and Phase~2 decides who gets surfaced from it. The experimental results of this dual-sided strategy are presented in Section~\ref{sec:exp_hybrid_results}.


\subsection{Phase 1: Job Recommendation with Bilateral Scoring}
\label{sec:hybrid_job_congestion}

In Phase~1, each user receives a ranked list of jobs. The ranking score
combines the user-side relevance signal with the recruiter-side suitability
signal, so that a job scores highly only if it is both a good match for the
user \emph{and} the user is a good candidate in the recruiter's eyes. The
Two-Tower logit $\hat{y}_{\text{TT}}$ is transformed to a probability
$p_{\text{TT}} = \sigma(\hat{y}_{\text{TT}}) \in [0,1]$, and the XGBoost
recruiter model produces a shortlisting probability $p_{\text{rec}} \in [0,1]$.
The combined ranking score for user $u$ on job $j$ is:
\begin{equation}
    s_{uj} = \alpha \cdot \bigl(p_{\text{TT}} +
    \delta_{\text{job}}\!\left(c_j^{(t)}\right)\bigr)
    + (1 - \alpha) \cdot p_{\text{rec}},
    \label{eq:combined_score}
\end{equation}
where $\delta_{\text{job}}(\cdot)$ is the job congestion modification from
Section~\ref{sec:job_congestion}, $c_j^{(t)}$ is the running application count
for job $j$ at the time user $u$ is processed, and $\alpha \in [0,1]$ is a
weighting parameter. Both signals are on the same $[0,1]$ probability scale,
making the weighted average well-defined and interpretable. The value of
$\alpha$ used in the experiments is 0.5 for simplicity.

The job congestion penalty is applied inside the Two-Tower component only. This is because applications happen in the user-to-job direction, which is the first stage of the pipeline. The recruiter probability is left unmodified, as candidate fit is
should be independent of how many other users happen to be applying to the same job.
Each user applies to their top-$k$ jobs by $s_{uj}$, where $k$ matches their
historical application count, preserving realistic application volumes.

A notable property of Equation~\ref{eq:combined_score} is that it reduces job congestion even without an explicit penalty ($δjob=0\delta_{\text{job}} = 0
δjob​=0$): for users with low recruiter suitability, the combined score is lower than the Two-Tower score alone, naturally promoting other jobs in their ranking. The empirical effect of this is examined in Section~\ref{sec:hybrid_bilateral_baseline}.

\subsection{Phase 2: recruiter shortlisting with candidate-side correction}
\label{sec:hybrid_user_congestion}

Phase~2 takes the job inboxes produced by Phase~1 and answers a different
question for a different audience: for each job, which candidates should the
recruiter shortlist? The recruiter model's suitability score $p_{\text{rec}}$
is reused from the Phase~1, but
now it ranks \emph{candidates within a job's inbox} rather than jobs for a
user. This is the opposite ranking direction from Phase~1.

The candidate-level market-maker modification from
Section~\ref{sec:user_congestion} is applied at this stage:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{r}_{uj} = p_{\text{rec}} +
    \delta_{\text{cand}}\!\left(v_u^{(t)}\right),
\end{equation}
where $v_u^{(t)}$ is the running count of how many times candidate $u$ has
already been shortlisted by previously processed job inboxes in this Phase~2
run. As the simulation progresses, candidates who have been shortlisted
frequently accumulate negative modifications, naturally redistributing
shortlisting opportunities toward candidates who have not yet been surfaced.
The top-$k$ candidates by $\hat{r}_{uj}$ are shortlisted for each job.

Candidate congestion is also reduced implicitly by the application pool
inherited from Phase~1. Because the combined score in Phase~1 filters out
applications where the candidate has low recruiter suitability, job inboxes are
smaller and more competitive. This produces more diverse shortlists in which
the recruiter's top-$k$ selection reaches a wider range of candidates than it
would from a larger, lower-quality applicant pool.

The output of Phase~2 is a distribution of shortlisting events per candidate,
which is evaluated using the Gini coefficient and zone classification defined
in Sections~\ref{sec:prelim_fairness} and~\ref{sec:prelim_zones}.
