\chapter{Market Congestion and Hybrid Balancing}
\label{chap:congestion_balancing}

Now that both the user-side recommendation system
(Chapter~\ref{chap:user_recommendation}) and the recruiter-side recommendation
system (Chapter~\ref{chap:recruiter_recommendation}) have been developed, the
next step is to mitigate the congestion that emerges as a consequence of
recommendation at scale. As introduced in Section~\ref{sec:prelim_twosided},
two distinct forms arise: job-side congestion, in which traffic concentrates on
a small subset of job postings, and user-side congestion, in which recruiter
attention concentrates on a small subset of candidates. Both forms are first
addressed in isolation using the Gini-based measurement framework and
market-maker intervention strategies defined in
Sections~\ref{sec:prelim_fairness} and~\ref{sec:prelim_zones}. The two
strategies are then combined into a dual-sided hybrid that balances the market
simultaneously. Experimental evaluation is deferred to
Chapter~\ref{chap:experiments}.

% ============================================================
\section{Job-Side Congestion}
\label{sec:job_congestion}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Job-Side Congestion in the Dataset}
\label{sec:job_congestion_dataset}

Job-side congestion is measured over the distribution of applications per job
posting, using the Gini coefficient $G(\mathbf{c})$ defined in
Equation~\ref{eq:gini} and the safe/starved/congested zone classification from
Section~\ref{sec:prelim_zones}. The zone boundaries are set to the median and
90th percentile of the empirical application-count distribution in Window~7,
yielding $\tau_{\min}^{\text{job}} = 2$ and $\tau_{\max}^{\text{job}} = 10$.

Applying the historical Window~7 application records to this framework reveals a
highly skewed distribution. Over 80\% of active job postings receive fewer than
two applications, while a small fraction attract tens or hundreds. The Gini
coefficient of the historical distribution is 0.8575, confirming extreme
concentration. This baseline reflects the natural self-selection behaviour of job
seekers: certain postings are intrinsically more appealing by title, location, or
employer brand, and attract disproportionate organic interest. A recommender
system operating without congestion awareness risks amplifying this imbalance
further, as the experimental results in Section~\ref{sec:exp_job_congestion}
demonstrate.

\subsection{Market-Maker Intervention}
\label{sec:job_congestion_intervention}

To redistribute application traffic more equitably, a market-maker modification
is applied to the job ranking score during the recommendation phase. At the
moment user $u$ is being processed, let $c_j^{(t)}$ denote the number of
applications job $j$ has already received from previously processed users in the
current simulation run. The modified ranking score for the $(u, j)$ pair is:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{s}_{uj} = s_{uj} + \delta\!\left(c_j^{(t)}\right),
\end{equation}
where $s_{uj}$ is the base relevance score and $\delta(\cdot)$ is a congestion
modification function. Jobs within the safe zone receive no modification
($\delta = 0$). Starved jobs receive a positive boost to increase their
visibility. Congested jobs receive a negative penalty to reduce further
applications. Jobs exceeding a hard cap $H^{\text{job}}$ are removed from the
ranking entirely. Three functional forms for $\delta$ are considered.

\paragraph{Linear modification.}
The penalty grows at a constant rate per unit of congestion beyond the safe
zone:
\begin{equation}
    \delta_{\text{lin}}(c) = \begin{cases}
        \min\!\bigl(\gamma_b \cdot (\tau_{\min} - c),\; B\bigr) & c < \tau_{\min}, \\
        0 & \tau_{\min} \leq c \leq \tau_{\max}, \\
        \max\!\bigl(-\gamma_p \cdot (c - \tau_{\max}),\; -P\bigr) & c > \tau_{\max},
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}
where $B$ and $P$ are the maximum boost and penalty magnitudes respectively,
and $\gamma_b$, $\gamma_p$ are the rates per unit of deviation from the safe
zone.

\paragraph{Quadratic modification.}
The penalty grows quadratically, starting slowly for mildly congested jobs and
accelerating as congestion increases:
\begin{equation}
    \delta_{\text{quad}}(c) = \begin{cases}
        \min\!\bigl(\kappa_b \cdot (\tau_{\min} - c)^2,\; B\bigr) & c < \tau_{\min}, \\
        0 & \tau_{\min} \leq c \leq \tau_{\max}, \\
        \max\!\bigl(-\kappa_p \cdot (c - \tau_{\max})^2,\; -P\bigr) & c > \tau_{\max},
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}
where $\kappa_b$ and $\kappa_p$ are scaling parameters.

\paragraph{Sigmoid modification.}
A smooth S-curve naturally bounds the modification within $[-P, B]$ without
requiring explicit clamping:
\begin{equation}
    \delta_{\text{sig}}(c) = \begin{cases}
        B \cdot \!\left(\dfrac{2}{1 + e^{-k(\tau_{\min} - c)}} - 1\right)
            & c < \tau_{\min}, \\[6pt]
        0 & \tau_{\min} \leq c \leq \tau_{\max}, \\[6pt]
        -P \cdot \!\left(\dfrac{2}{1 + e^{-k(c - \tau_{\max})}} - 1\right)
            & c > \tau_{\max},
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}
where $k$ controls the steepness of the curve.

All three functions share the same boundary values ($B$, $P$, $H^{\text{job}}$)
but differ in how quickly the modification grows between the safe zone and the
hard cap. The linear form reaches its maximum penalty after a fixed number of
applications beyond $\tau_{\max}^{\text{job}}$. The quadratic form is lenient on
moderate congestion but aggressive once jobs are heavily over-applied. The
sigmoid form provides a smooth transition that saturates gradually. The parameter
values used throughout all experiments are listed in
Table~\ref{tab:modification_params_job}.

\section{User-Side Congestion}
\label{sec:user_congestion}
% ============================================================

\subsection{User-Side Congestion in the Dataset}
\label{sec:user_congestion_dataset}

User-side congestion is measured over the distribution of shortlisting events
per candidate, using the Gini coefficient $G(\mathbf{v})$ and the zone
classification from Section~\ref{sec:prelim_zones}. The thresholds are set to
$\tau_{\min}^{\text{cand}} = 1$ and $\tau_{\max}^{\text{cand}} = 5$,
corresponding to the recruiter's per-job screen size of top-5.

Simulating recruiter shortlisting on the historical Window~7 application pool —
scoring each applicant with the XGBoost recruiter model and selecting the top-5
per job — reveals a substantially unequal distribution of attention. In the
free-market setting, 15.8\% of candidates receive zero shortlisting events
despite having applied to at least one job, while 9.1\% are shortlisted across
more than five jobs simultaneously. The Gini coefficient is 0.5393. This
baseline reflects two interacting forces: the natural skew in candidate quality
as assessed by the recruiter model, and the concentration of applications on
popular postings that brings the same strong candidates into contact with many
recruiters simultaneously. The experimental results in
Section~\ref{sec:exp_user_congestion} evaluate the effectiveness of the
market-maker strategies against this baseline.

\subsection{Market-Maker Intervention}
\label{sec:user_congestion_intervention}

The same three functional forms applied to job-side congestion are adapted for
the candidate side. At the moment job $j$'s inbox is being processed, let
$v_u^{(t)}$ denote the number of times candidate $u$ has already been
shortlisted in the current simulation run. The modified recruiter ranking score
is:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{r}_{uj} = r_{uj} + \delta_{\text{cand}}\!\left(v_u^{(t)}\right),
\end{equation}
where $r_{uj}$ is the recruiter model's base suitability probability and
$\delta_{\text{cand}}(\cdot)$ uses the same functional forms as
Section~\ref{sec:job_congestion_intervention} but parameterised with
candidate-side thresholds. The top-$k$ candidates by $\hat{r}_{uj}$ are
shortlisted. As more job inboxes are processed, candidates who have been
shortlisted frequently accumulate negative modifications, naturally redirecting
attention toward candidates who have not yet been surfaced. The candidate-side
parameter values are listed in Table~\ref{tab:modification_params_cand}.

\section{Dual-Sided Hybrid Strategy}
\label{sec:hybrid_strategy}
% ============================================================

The job-side and user-side mitigations described above address each form of
congestion in isolation, treating a single model's scores as the sole ranking
signal. A more principled approach recognises that both forms of congestion
share a common root: the scoring function considers only one side of the market
at a time. The Two-Tower model reflects user preferences but is blind to whether
the candidate is actually competitive for the role. The recruiter model reflects
employer preferences but plays no role in shaping which users apply in the first
place. The dual-sided hybrid strategy addresses both congestions simultaneously
by combining both signals into a single bilateral score, and applies the
market-maker modification to the appropriate component on each side.

\subsection{Part A: Reducing Job Congestion with Bilateral Scoring}
\label{sec:hybrid_job_congestion}

In the hybrid strategy, the score used to rank jobs for each user incorporates
both the user-side relevance signal and the recruiter-side suitability signal
before the job-side market-maker modification is applied. The Two-Tower logit
$\hat{y}_{\text{TT}}$ is transformed to a probability $p_{\text{TT}} =
\sigma(\hat{y}_{\text{TT}}) \in [0,1]$, and the XGBoost recruiter model
produces a shortlisting probability $p_{\text{rec}} \in [0,1]$. The combined
ranking score for user $u$ on job $j$ is:
\begin{equation}
    s_{uj} = \alpha \cdot \bigl(p_{\text{TT}} +
    \delta_{\text{job}}\!\left(c_j^{(t)}\right)\bigr)
    + (1 - \alpha) \cdot p_{\text{rec}},
    \label{eq:combined_score}
\end{equation}
where $\delta_{\text{job}}(\cdot)$ is the job congestion modification from
Section~\ref{sec:job_congestion_intervention} and $\alpha \in [0,1]$ is a
weighting parameter. Both signals are on the same $[0,1]$ probability scale,
making the weighted average well-defined and interpretable.

The job congestion penalty is applied inside the Two-Tower component only. This
is by design: job overflow is driven by users applying to popular jobs from
their own perspective, so the correction acts on the user-side signal. The
recruiter probability is left unmodified, as candidate fit is independent of how
many other users are applying to the same job. After scoring, each user applies
to their top-$k$ jobs by $s_{uj}$, simulating the real-world process in which
users engage with a ranked recommendation list.

A notable emergent property of Equation~\ref{eq:combined_score} is that it
reduces job congestion even without an explicit penalty ($\delta_{\text{job}} =
0$). Popular jobs attract many users because they score highly on the Two-Tower
model; however, among those users, only a subset are genuinely competitive
candidates as assessed by the recruiter model. For users with low recruiter
suitability, the combined score is lower than the Two-Tower score alone,
promoting other jobs in their ranking. The congestion reduction is therefore a
natural consequence of bilateral quality filtering, prior to and independent of
the market-maker mechanism.

\subsection{Part B: Reducing User Congestion via Recruiter Shortlisting}
\label{sec:hybrid_user_congestion}

Given the application pool produced by Part~A, the recruiter evaluates each
job's inbox and shortlists the top-$k$ candidates. The candidate-level
market-maker modification from Section~\ref{sec:user_congestion_intervention}
is applied at this stage:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{r}_{uj} = p_{\text{rec}} +
    \delta_{\text{cand}}\!\left(v_u^{(t)}\right).
\end{equation}
As the simulation progresses through more job inboxes, candidates who have been
shortlisted frequently accumulate penalties, naturally redistributing
shortlisting opportunities toward candidates who have not yet been surfaced.

Candidate congestion is also reduced implicitly by the application pool from
Part~A. Because the combined score filters out applications where the candidate
has low recruiter suitability, job inboxes are smaller and more competitive.
This creates more diverse shortlists in which the recruiter's top-$k$ selection
reaches a wider range of candidates than it would from a larger, lower-quality
applicant pool.

\subsection{Part C: Combined Market Equilibrium}
\label{sec:hybrid_balance}

The full dual-sided hybrid strategy operates as a sequential two-phase pipeline.

\textbf{Phase 1 — Job Recommendation.} For each user, jobs are ranked using the
combined score from Equation~\ref{eq:combined_score}, with the job congestion
modification active. The user applies to their top-$k$ jobs, where $k$ matches
their historical application count. The output is a set of (user, job)
application pairs and a distribution of applications per job.

\textbf{Phase 2 — Recruiter Shortlisting.} The job inboxes produced by Phase~1
are processed sequentially. For each inbox, applicants are ranked by the
modified recruiter score $\hat{r}_{uj}$ and the top-5 candidates are
shortlisted. The output is a distribution of shortlisting events per candidate.

Both phases use the same market-maker strategy within a given simulation run:
if the linear modification is active for job congestion in Phase~1, the linear
modification is also active for candidate congestion in Phase~2. The weighting
parameter $\alpha = 0.5$ is used throughout, giving equal weight to both models.
The experimental validation of this pipeline is presented in
Section~\ref{sec:exp_hybrid_results}.
