\chapter{Experiments and Results}
\label{chap:experiments}
This chapter describes the experimental evaluation of the systems developed in previous chapters. First, the shared experiment settings are described in Section~\ref{sec:exp_settings} which includes dataset statistics, how the temporal split is applied, and a summary of the hardware configuration which was used. Then, experiments are presented for the user-side recommender system (Section~\ref{sec:exp_user}), the recruiter-side recommender system (Section~\ref{sec:exp_recruiter}), and the congestion balancing strategies (Section~\ref{sec:exp_congestion}).

\section{General Experiments Setting}
\label{sec:exp_settings}

\subsection{Dataset}
\label{sec:exp_dataset}
All experiments are conducted on the CareerBuilder Job Recommendation dataset~\cite{careerbuilderdataset}, consisting of real-world job applications, user profiles and job postings from CareerBuilder.com over a 13-week period. The dataset is organised into seven consecutive 13-day temporal windows where users and jobs are assigned to a single window. Each user applies to jobs which are active in the same window. Table~\ref{tab:dataset_stats} summarises the scale of the full dataset.

\begin{table}[htbp]

The extremely high sparsity --- fewer than one in 200,000 user-job pairs has a recorded interaction --- means that collaborative approaches relying on interaction density are infeasible. The two-tower architecture instead uses interactions as a training signal while generalising through user and job profile features, making it robust to sparse data.

Rather than using the original within-window train/test labels provided 
by the dataset, a temporal split across windows is applied, as 
illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:custom_temporal_split}. Window~7 
represents the target deployment period --- the future time window that 
all models in this thesis are designed to serve. No model is trained on Window~7 data; it is reserved as the sanity check and production 
window for all models. Windows~1--6 
provide the historical data from which each component learns and is 
developed, though the exact partitioning differs by component and is
described in the respective experiment sections.

Users marked \texttt{Split=Test} in the original dataset are excluded 
entirely (22,838 users). These users made five or more applications 
during the withheld 4-day test period of each window. Of these, 43\% 
have no recorded interactions in the 9-day training period whatsoever, 
meaning their only observable activity falls within the withheld 
portion. Including them would introduce users with incomplete or 
entirely missing interaction records into the evaluation, so they 
are excluded entirely.

An important consequence of this temporal split is that all users in Window~7 are cold-start users with no recorded interactions in the training period. This is a realistic reflection of the production setting, where every user at inference time is new to the model. This makes the evaluation more challenging but also more meaningful. \subsection{Hardware and Software Configuration}
\label{sec:hardware}

All experiments are run on the Artificial Intelligence and Data Analytics Research Group (AIDA) high-performance computing cluster via SSH. Training is performed on a single NVIDIA A40 GPU (46~GB VRAM). The software stack uses Python with PyTorch as the deep learning framework, scikit-learn for the lightweight recruiter-side models, and the Sentence Transformers library for Qwen3 text embeddings.

\section{User-Side Job Recommender System Experiments}\label{sec:exp_user}
% ============================================================
 
\subsection{Experiment goal}
\label{sec:exp_user_goal}
The goal of the user-side experiments is to train and evaluate a 
two-tower model that can rank relevant jobs highly for cold-start users 
--- users with no interaction history in the training period.  
Success is measured primarily by \gls{AUC-ROC}, as this metric is least 
sensitive to the candidate pool size of the validation set. The practical 
utility of the model is additionally assessed qualitatively at inference 
time by inspecting recommendations for a sample of Window~7 users against 
the full Window~7 job pool.

\subsection{Experiment setting}
\label{sec:exp_user_setting}

Windows~1--4 are used for training, Windows~5--6 for validation, and 
Window~7 serves as the qualitative evaluation window, where the model 
is applied to entirely new users and jobs as it would be in production. This way we keep the temporal window split described in Section~\ref{sec:custom_split}.

\subsection{Dataset creation}
From the 189,657 users in the training set, a random sample of 25,000 users per window is drawn to create a manageable training set of 100,000 users. With a median of 2 applications per user, this results in approximately 200,000 positive user-job pairs for training. The validation set is not subsampled and contains all 78,428 users having approximately 156,856 positive user-job pairs for validation. For each positive pair $(u, j^+)$, four negatives are sampled according 
to a \textbf{2:1:1 ratio}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{2 cross-state negatives}: jobs from states other than the user's own state, sampled from the same temporal window. These teach the model that geographic fit is a genuine signal.
    \item \textbf{1 same-state negative}: a job from the user's state but a different city, active in the same window. This forces the model to look beyond state-level location and discriminate on career content.
    \item \textbf{1 same-city negative}: a job posted in the user's city but one the user did not apply to. These are the hardest negatives, as they share the user's location and are still unapplied to, pressuring the model to learn semantic fit.
\end{itemize}
 
The 2:1:1 ratio reflects the geographic structure of the real inference 
setting: when ranking a user against the full job pool, the vast majority 
of candidates are in other states, a smaller fraction share the user's 
state, and only a handful are in the same city. Training with a matching 
distribution ensures the model learns to discriminate at each geographic 
granularity rather than exploiting an artificial balance. The effect of 
this is confirmed qualitatively in Section~\ref{sec:exp_user_inference}.

\subsection{Hyperparameters and Training Settings}
\label{sec:exp_user_hyperparameters}

Each categorical feature is mapped to a 16-dimensional embedding and 
the text embedding is truncated to $d_{\text{text}} = 100$. 
Table~\ref{tab:tower_input_dims} shows the resulting input dimension 
breakdown: text accounts for approximately 51\% of the user tower 
input and 76\% of the job tower input — large enough to carry the 
semantic signal without suppressing the structured features.

Both towers share the same MLP structure: two hidden layers of 512 and 
256 units, followed by the $d$-dimensional output layer, giving a 
202→512→256→64 reduction for the user tower and 134→512→256→64 for 
the job tower. Batch normalisation and ReLU are applied after each 
hidden layer, with dropout rates of 0.3 and 0.2 for the first and 
second hidden layer respectively. The output dimension is $d = 64$.

The model is trained for a maximum of 50 epochs with early stopping 
(patience~= 5) based on validation loss, using the Adam optimiser 
with a learning rate of $10^{-3}$ and a batch size of 1024.

\subsection{Ablation study}
\label{sec:exp_user_ablation}
 
To determine the optimal model configuration, a systematic ablation study is conducted comparing two design choices: whether to include location features (city and state) and whether to apply L2 normalisation to the output embeddings. All configurations are trained with the 2:1:1 negative sampling strategy described in Section~\ref{sec:negative_sampling} and use the same training settings described in Section~\ref{sec:exp_user_hyperparameters}. Table~\ref{tab:ablation_user} reports the results at the best validation checkpoint for each configuration.
 
A note on interpreting the ranking metrics: Hit@K, Recall@K and NDCG@K are computed against the validation set's balanced candidate pool (positive and sampled negative jobs per user) rather than the full job catalogue. For example if a user has applied to 2 jobs, the metrics are computed against a pool of 2 positive jobs and 8 sampled negative jobs using the negative sampling strategy. As discussed in Section~\ref{sec:prelim_metrics}, this makes their absolute values considerably more optimistic than what would be observed at full-scale inference. \gls{AUC-ROC} is therefore used as the primary comparison criterion, as it is less sensitive to this pool-size effect.
 
\begin{table}[htbp]
    \centering
    \caption{Ablation study results at best validation checkpoint, early stopping (patience~= 5).}
    \label{tab:ablation_user}
    \begin{tabular}{llcccc}
        \toprule
        \textbf{Location Features} & \textbf{L2 Norm.} & \textbf{Val Loss} & \textbf{Accuracy} & \textbf{AUC} & \textbf{Epochs} \\
        \midrule
        Included & No  & 0.2706 & 88.90\% & \textbf{0.9242} & 19 \\
        Excluded & No  & 0.3463 & 84.92\% & 0.8641 &  9 \\

        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}
 
Two findings emerge from the results. FFirst, \textbf{L2 normalisation consistently hurts performance} regardless 
of whether location features are present — AUC drops from 0.9242 to 0.7331 
with location included, and from 0.8641 to 0.7426 without. This is an interesting finding considering that cosine similarity is a common practice to calculate embedding similarity \cite{you2025semanticsanglecosinesimilarity}. Normalising output embeddings to unit norm removes their magnitude, which prevents the model from encoding match confidence in the scale of the dot product. Without normalisation, the magnitude acts as an additional degree of freedom that the model can use to express certainty, benefiting both the loss and the ranking quality.
 
Second, \textbf{including location features improves performance substantially} — \gls{AUC-ROC} rises from 0.8641 to 0.9242. The 2:1:1 strategy exposes the model to geographically diverse negatives during training. This prevents the model from learning city-density patterns and instead allows it to genuinely learn state-level geographic preference as a meaningful signal. The result is that the location-included model not only scores higher 
offline but also produces geographically sensible recommendations at 
inference, as illustrated in Section~\ref{sec:exp_user_inference}.
 
Based on the quantitative results, the final model \textbf{includes 
location features and applies no L2 normalisation}, a choice further 
supported by the qualitative evaluation in 
Section~\ref{sec:exp_user_inference}. A complete breakdown of all four 
configurations across all ranking metrics is provided in 
Table~\ref{app:tab:full_ablation} in the Appendix.

at inference time against all jobs from Window~7 (approximately 40,000 
jobs) for a sample of test users. Two criteria are used to assess the 
output: whether recommended jobs are semantically consistent with the 
user's career history if available, and whether the geographic distribution of 
recommendations is coherent with the user's location.

\section{Recruiter-side candidate recommender system experiments}
\label{sec:exp_recruiter}
% ============================================================
 
\subsection{Experiment goal}
\label{sec:exp_recruiter_goal}
 
The recruiter-side experiments address two design choices that must be made before a final model can be deployed. The first is \textbf{classifier selection}: given the engineered feature set described in Section~\ref{sec:recruiter_features}, which model family best exploits it? The second is \textbf{negative strategy selection}: given the chosen classifier, does the choice of negative construction — \gls{LLM}-evaluated hard negatives from the applicant pool, synthetically sampled non-applicants or a combination of both — have a measurable effect on ranking quality, and which strategy yields the more useful model? These two questions are answered sequentially in Sections~\ref{sec:exp_recruiter_model_comparison} and~\ref{sec:exp_recruiter_negatives}. Success is assessed both quantitatively via the metrics introduced in Section~\ref{sec:prelim_classification}, and qualitatively through sanity checks.
 
\subsection{Experiment setting}
\label{sec:exp_recruiter_setting}

LLM-labelled pairs are drawn from Windows~1--4, these pairs are split into a training, validation and test set.
Window~7 is not used for training or model selection; it serves
exclusively as the deployment window for the qualitative sanity check
and the congestion balancing simulation of
Chapter~\ref{chap:congestion_balancing}. 

\subsection{Dataset creation}
\label{sec:exp_recruiter_datagen}
Unlike the two-tower model, which derives its training signal directly
from observed applications, the recruiter-side model requires an
additional labeling step before any dataset can be constructed. The
labeling procedure is described in Section~\ref{sec:recruiter_data_gen};
this section covers the implementation details.

Labels are generated using the Qwen3.5~(35B) model served locally via
the Ollama API. For computational feasibility, a sample of jobs is drawn
from Windows~1--4; for each job, up to 20 applicants are evaluated in a
single batched prompt. The final prompt used is:

{\small
\begin{verbatim}
Task: You are an HR recruiter. Evaluate how well each person fits the
job based on their profile and the job description.
(IMPORTANT: Treat each job-user pair independently.)

Assign a fit level:
- "Very Good Fit": Meets 100% of Must-Haves AND 50%+ of Nice-to-Haves
  AND 0 Deal-Breakers.
- "Good Fit": Meets 100% of Must-Haves (OR misses 1 Must-Have but
  compensates with extensive relevant experience) AND 0 Deal-Breakers.
- "Poor Fit": Missing 2+ Must-Haves OR has exactly 1 Deal-Breaker.
- "Very Poor Fit": Missing 3+ Must-Haves OR has 2+ Deal-Breakers.

Return a JSON object:
{"results": [{"candidate_id": <id>, "must_haves": "...",
  "nice_to_haves": "...", "deal_breakers": "...",
  "reasoning": "...", "fit_level": "Good Fit"}]}

Notes:
- Be strict. Only candidates that truly meet requirements get Good Fit.
- Be objective. Focus on concrete matches/mismatches.
- If Degree, Major, Experience or History is empty/NaN, assume the
  candidate does not have that feature.
- Assume all applicants are 18+, available, and physically able.

Job Description: {job_desc}
Candidate Profiles: {profiles_text}
\end{verbatim}
}

The prompt enforces consistency through three mechanisms: explicit
fit level rules that remove threshold ambiguity, a structured JSON
output format that requires the model to state must-haves, deal-breakers
and a reasoning trace before assigning a label, and targeted notes
addressing failure modes observed during iterative prompt development.
The most significant of these was the treatment of missing values:
without explicit instruction, the model would assume absent profile
fields indicated average candidates rather than missing information,
producing overly optimistic fit levels. Responses that fail JSON
parsing or contain unexpected fit level values are discarded.

An example output for a single candidate is shown below:

jobs. Table~\ref{tab:recruiter_label_dist} shows the fit level

many applicants lacking one or more stated must-haves. Class weights
are applied during training inversely proportional to class frequency.

\subsection{Stage 2: Negative Strategy Selection}
\label{sec:exp_recruiter_negatives}
 
Having established XGBoost as the strongest classifier, the model type is now held fixed and the negative construction strategy is varied. Table~\ref{tab:recruiter_neg_comparison} reports XGBoost test set performance for each strategy, where each model is evaluated on a test set constructed with the same negative type as its training data.
 
\subsubsection{Synthetic vs.\ LLM negatives}
The first interesting but expected result is the gap between the synthetic and LLM models: \gls{AUC} 0.852 vs.\ 0.724, a difference of 0.128. This gap is a task-difficulty effect. The synthetic model is tested on random non-applicants, who by construction are unlikely to match the job on any meaningful feature; almost any signal separates them from strong applicants. The LLM model is tested on poor-fit applicants from the real pool --- candidates who self-selected to apply despite a mismatch. These share the same self-selection signal as positives, forcing the model to rely on deeper feature differences. The lower \gls{AUC} correctly reflects the genuine difficulty of that discrimination task.
 
\subsubsection{Combined as the best choice}
The combined model achieves \gls{AUC} 0.755 on a mixed test set with equal proportions of LLM and synthetic negatives, outperforming the LLM-only model (0.724) but underperforming the synthetic-only model (0.852). Crucially, in the congestion balancing context of Chapter~\ref{chap:congestion_balancing}, the model must score candidates who may not have applied to the job at all --- which is precisely the synthetic scenario. A model trained exclusively on LLM negatives has never seen that setting and may not generalise well to it. The combined model handles both regimes and achieves the best verified performance across them.

\section{Congestion Balancing Experiments}
\label{sec:exp_congestion}

\subsection{Experiment Goal}
\label{sec:exp_congestion_goal}
 
The congestion balancing experiments pursue three goals in sequence. The first
is to demonstrate that both forms of congestion described in
Chapter~\ref{chap:congestion_balancing} are genuinely present in the data,
before any intervention is applied and to form a baseline for the subsequent experiments. The second is to show that the job-side and
candidate-side market-maker strategies each independently reduce their respective
form of congestion while preserving acceptable match quality. The third is to show that the dual-sided hybrid strategy from
Section~\ref{sec:hybrid_strategy} addresses both forms simultaneously.
 
\subsection{Experiment Setting}
\label{sec:exp_congestion_setting}
\paragraph{Simulation arena.}
The congestion balancing experiments operate exclusively on Window~7, 
the deployment window introduced in Section~\ref{sec:custom_split}. 
Both the Two-Tower model and the recruiter-side model are 
applied here without any further fine-tuning. Window~7 thus serves as 
a held-out production simulation and also as baseline comparison. The market-maker strategies are 
evaluated on the resulting recommendation and shortlisting distributions.

 
\paragraph{Parameter values.}
Tables~\ref{tab:job_mm_params} and~\ref{tab:cand_mm_params} list the
market-maker parameter values used in all congestion experiments. As with the
thresholds, these are design choices rather than optimised hyperparameters:
the boost and penalty magnitudes are set to be on the same $[0,1]$ probability
scale as the base scores they modify. Because both sides share the same lower
threshold $\tau_{\min} = 2$, the starved zone spans an identical range of two
units on both sides, and the boost parameters ($\gamma_b$, $\kappa_b$, $k_b$)
are consequently the same for both the job-side and candidate-side
market-makers. The penalty shape parameters ($\gamma_p$, $\kappa_p$, $k_p$)
differ between the two sides because the congested zones have different ranges:
the job-side hard cap is $H^{\text{job}}=50$, giving a penalty range of 40
units beyond $\tau_{\max}^{\text{job}}=9$, whereas the candidate-side hard cap
is $H^{\text{cand}}=10$, giving a penalty range of only 3 units beyond
$\tau_{\max}^{\text{cand}}=6$. In both cases the penalty parameters are
calibrated so that the smooth penalty reaches $P=-0.25$ at $H-1$ (i.e.\
49 applications for the job side, 9 shortlists for the candidate side).
At $H$ itself, a penalty of $-\infty$ is applied, effectively
removing the entity from consideration entirely.

Figures~\ref{fig:modification_functions} and~\ref{fig:modification_functions_cand}
illustrate the three functional forms for the job side and candidate side
respectively. On the job side, where the congested zone spans 40 units, the
three curves diverge clearly: the sigmoid descends steeply and saturates near
its maximum penalty well before the hard cap, making it the most aggressive
redistributor for moderately congested jobs; the quadratic starts slowly but
accelerates as congestion grows; and the linear descends at a constant rate,
reaching its maximum only at $H^{\text{job}}-1 = 49$. On the candidate side,
the compressed range of 3 units causes all three curves to converge rapidly
to $-0.25$, leaving little practical difference between them beyond the speed
of initial descent.

\paragraph{Simulation mechanics.}
For the job-side and hybrid experiments, Two-Tower recommendations and
recruiter scores are pre-computed and cached before any strategy is run, so
that all four strategy variants see identical model outputs and differ only in
how those outputs are combined and modified. Users and job inboxes are processed
sequentially; the running counters $c_j^{(t)}$ and $v_u^{(t)}$ are reset
between strategy runs. The weighting parameter $\alpha = 0.5$ is used in
all hybrid experiments for simplicity, giving equal weight to the Two-Tower and recruiter signals.
 
\paragraph{Metrics.}
Congestion is measured using the Gini coefficient $G$ and the percentage of entities in each zone (starved, safe, congested) as
defined in Section~\ref{sec:prelim_zones}. To assess whether congestion
reduction comes at the cost of match quality, the average Two-Tower probability
$\bar{p}_{\text{TT}}$ and average recruiter probability $\bar{p}_{\text{rec}}$
are also reported across all applied (user, job) pairs. A strategy that reduces
congestion while keeping these averages close to the free-market baseline is
considered successful.

\paragraph{Difference between analyses.} One further distinction applies within the congestion experiments 
themselves. The standalone job-side and candidate-side analyses 
(Sections~\ref{sec:exp_job_congestion} and~\ref{sec:exp_user_congestion}) 
operate on different input populations. The job-side analysis uses 
applications generated by the Two-Tower recommender, since the 
market-maker intervenes in the recommendation step itself. The 
standalone candidate analysis uses the \emph{real} application pool 
from Window~7, isolating the recruiter re-ranking mechanism without 
any confounding from the upstream recommender. The hybrid experiment 
(Section~\ref{sec:exp_hybrid_results}) then combines both: 
Two-Tower-generated applications form the job inboxes, and the 
recruiter re-ranks within those inboxes. As a consequence, the 
candidate congestion baseline in the hybrid section 
(Table~\ref{tab:hybrid_cand_results}) differs from the standalone 
candidate baseline (Table~\ref{tab:user_congestion_baseline}) --- 
the Two-Tower recommender concentrates applications more unevenly than 
organic self-selection, so the hybrid starting point is a more 
unequal distribution. The two candidate analyses are therefore 
complementary rather than directly comparable: the standalone 
section demonstrates the re-ranking mechanism in isolation, and 
the hybrid section evaluates it in the full production pipeline.

 \subsubsection{Market-maker simulation results}
Table~\ref{tab:job_mm_results} compares all strategies on Window~7. The first
notable finding is that the free-market Two-Tower recommender makes congestion
\emph{worse} than the historical baseline: the Gini rises from 0.8575 to
0.9625 and the starved fraction increases from 80.9\% to 90.8\%. This is
expected --- the Two-Tower model optimises for relevance and will confidently
direct many users towards the same popular jobs, amplifying the feedback loop
rather than correcting it. This result motivates the market-maker intervention
directly: an uncorrected recommender is not a neutral system; it actively
concentrates traffic further.

All three market-maker strategies produce a large improvement over the free
market. The Gini drops from 0.9625 to between 0.5741 and 0.5994 depending on
the strategy, and the safe zone grows from 6.4\% to between 23.8\% and 46.2\%.

The three functional forms produce meaningfully different distributions. The
sigmoid strategy achieves the best overall balance: it has the lowest Gini
(0.5741), the smallest starved fraction (51.3\%), and the largest safe zone
(46.2\%). The linear strategy ranks second on Gini (0.5849) and starved
fraction (63.4\%), outperforming the quadratic on both metrics. The quadratic
strategy has the highest Gini (0.5994) and the largest starved fraction
(74.2\%), which is explained by its design: the quadratic penalty is lenient
on mildly congested jobs but becomes aggressive on heavily congested ones,
pulling down the extreme tail while doing less to help the mildly
under-applied. The linear strategy, by contrast, applies a steady uniform
penalty that redistributes traffic more evenly across the full congested range.

The match quality cost is modest across all strategies. The average Two-Tower
probability drops from 0.9537 under free market to between 0.8837 and 0.8963
--- a reduction of 6--7\%. The average recruiter probability also decreases
slightly (0.4976 free market vs 0.4737--0.4788 with penalties), a reduction of
around 4\%. This indicates that from the recruiter's perspective the suitability
of applied candidates is largely preserved despite the redistribution.

minor variants of the same accounts-payable role in Houston (AP Specialist,

problems: job-side congestion is driven by a recommendation feedback loop
that creates a wide, heavy tail the penalty curves can act across, while
candidate-side congestion is structurally bounded by individual application
behaviour and cannot develop the same runaway dynamic.

\subsection{Hybrid Balancing Results}
\label{sec:exp_hybrid_results}

As discussed in Section~\ref{sec:hybrid_strategy}, the hybrid experiment evaluates the full dual-sided pipeline, where the
Two-Tower recommender and the recruiter model operate jointly across two
phases. In the first phase, users are scored against jobs using a combined score that sums the logit outputs of both models. The weighting parameter $\alpha = 0.5$ is used for simplicity, giving
equal weight to both signals. The Two-Tower term has an optional penalty term. 

In Phase~2, each job's inbox — formed by
the applications from Phase~1 — is ranked by the recruiter model, optionally
modified by a candidate-side congestion penalty $\delta_{\text{cand}}(v_u)$
based on how many times candidate $u$ has already been shortlisted. The
top-5 candidates per job are shortlisted.

Four universes are evaluated in an ablation design, using the sigmoid
functional form throughout since it produced the best results in the
standalone job-side experiments
(Section~\ref{sec:exp_job_congestion}):

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Bilateral scoring, no penalties} — the combined
    $\alpha$-weighted score drives applications; the recruiter ranks
    inboxes without any congestion penalty. This isolates the effect of
    bilateral scoring alone.
    \item \textbf{Job-side sigmoid only} — the job congestion penalty
    $\delta_{\text{job}}$ is active in Phase~1; no candidate penalty in
    Phase~2.
    \item \textbf{Candidate-side sigmoid only} — no job penalty in
    Phase~1; the candidate congestion penalty $\delta_{\text{cand}}$ is
    active in Phase~2.
    \item \textbf{Both sigmoid} — both penalties active simultaneously.
    This is the complete dual-sided market-maker.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Bilateral scoring baseline}
\label{sec:hybrid_bilateral_baseline}
Table~\ref{tab:hybrid_job_results} shows that bilateral scoring alone
already improves over the pure Two-Tower free market from
Section~\ref{sec:exp_job_congestion}: the job-side Gini drops from
0.9625 to 0.9182, and the starved fraction decreases from 90.8\% to
85.6\%. This improvement requires no explicit congestion penalty —
it arises purely from incorporating the recruiter signal into the
application ranking. Because the recruiter score captures candidate-job
fit from a different perspective than the Two-Tower, the combined score
diversifies which jobs users apply to, partially breaking the feedback
loop that concentrates traffic on popular postings.

The match quality picture is particularly striking. Compared to the
standalone Two-Tower free market ($\bar{p}_{\text{TT}} = 0.9537$), the
bilateral baseline shows a modest drop in average TT probability to
0.8966 (Table~\ref{tab:hybrid_scores}) — a reduction of 5.9\%. However, the average recruiter
probability rises significantly from 0.5711 to 0.8405. This is not a
contradiction: the TT model was optimised purely for relevance and
its top recommendations are jobs the user is likely to apply to, but
not necessarily jobs where the recruiter would consider them a strong
fit. By weighting the recruiter signal equally alongside the TT score,
the combined ranking steers users toward jobs where they are both
interested and genuinely suitable — accepting a small reduction in
application likelihood in exchange for a large gain in candidate-job
fit quality from the recruiter's perspective.

On the candidate side (Table~\ref{tab:hybrid_cand_results}), the
bilateral baseline produces a Gini of 0.6326 with 32.1\% of candidates
starved. This is considerably worse than the standalone user-side
baseline (Gini 0.5370, 52.3\% starved) because the population here
is different: the standalone simulation operated on the real application
pool where candidates self-selected into jobs. Here, the TT recommender
directs users toward jobs the model believes are relevant, which
concentrates applications — and therefore recruiter attention — more
unevenly than organic behaviour.

\subsubsection{Ablation: isolating each penalty}
\label{sec:hybrid_ablation}
\paragraph{Job-side penalty only.}
Adding the job-side sigmoid penalty reduces the job Gini substantially
from 0.9182 to 0.7276, and grows the safe zone from 11.1\% to 29.4\%.
However, this improvement is considerably smaller than in the standalone
job-side experiment, where the same sigmoid penalty achieved a Gini of
0.5985. The gap is explained by the structure of the combined scoring
function (Equation~\ref{eq:hybrid_score}): the job penalty
$\delta_{\text{job}}$ only acts on the Two-Tower component, which
carries weight $\alpha = 0.5$. The recruiter probability term is
congestion-unaware and pulls the combined score in the opposite
direction — a user may still apply to a congested job if the recruiter
score is high enough to outweigh the penalty. This dilution is a
fundamental consequence of the bilateral design: the recruiter signal
improves match quality but attenuates the market-maker's redistribution
power. A higher $\alpha$ would strengthen the penalty at the cost of
recruiter influence, and represents a tunable trade-off left for future
work.

Notably, despite the redistribution cost, the recruiter probability
remains substantially higher than the standalone Two-Tower free market
baseline (Table~\ref{tab:hybrid_scores}), confirming that the
bilateral scoring gain in candidate-job fit quality is preserved even
after the job penalty is applied. The TT probability is lower than the
standalone baseline (0.8671 vs 0.9537), which is expected — the
bilateral score never optimises for TT probability alone, and the job
penalty pushes this further by redirecting users away from their
highest-ranked jobs.

The candidate-side effect of the job penalty is striking: starved
candidates drop from 32.1\% to just 6.8\%, and the candidate Gini
falls from 0.6326 to 0.5256. This improvement is entirely indirect —
by redistributing applications more evenly across jobs, each job inbox
contains a more diverse set of candidates, so recruiter attention
naturally spreads. The trade-off is a rise in congested candidates from
6.7\% to 13.7\%: when the job penalty pushes applications toward
previously empty jobs, those jobs now have small inboxes where all
applicants are automatically shortlisted, including candidates who are
already frequently shortlisted elsewhere.

\paragraph{Candidate-side penalty only.}
The candidate-side penalty applied without any job penalty produces a
modest improvement on the candidate side: Gini drops from 0.6326 to
0.5884 and starved candidates from 32.1\% to 27.9\%. The job-side
distribution is unchanged from the bilateral baseline by design — the
candidate penalty operates only in Phase~2 and has no influence on
which jobs users apply to in Phase~1. The match quality scores are the also equal for the same reason. The candidate-side improvement
here is smaller than the job-side penalty's indirect effect (Gini 0.5884
vs 0.5256), confirming that redistributing the application pool in
Phase~1 is a more powerful lever for candidate equity than re-ranking
within fixed inboxes in Phase~2.

\subsubsection{Full dual-sided market-maker}
\label{sec:full_pipeline}
Universe~4 combines both penalties and achieves the best results across
all metrics. On the job side, the Gini and zone fractions are identical
to universe~2 (0.7276, 29.4\% safe) — as expected, since the candidate
penalty operates in Phase~2 and cannot influence Phase~1 job
distributions. On the candidate side, the full system achieves a Gini
of 0.4950 with only 3.9\% of candidates starved and 82.9\% in the safe
zone. Compared to the bilateral baseline this represents a reduction of
28.2\% in the starved fraction.

The congested fraction remains elevated at 13.2\%, marginally below the
13.7\% of universe~2. The candidate penalty suppresses repeat shortlisting
but cannot fully counteract the structural effect of the job penalty
pushing applications into small inboxes. This is an inherent tension in
the dual-sided design: the mechanisms that most effectively reduce
candidate starvation also create pathways for mild candidate congestion.

Match quality scores are identical to universe~2
(Table~\ref{tab:hybrid_scores}),
as expected — the candidate-side penalty operates exclusively in
Phase~2 and has no influence on which jobs users apply to in Phase~1.
The match quality cost of the full system is therefore entirely
attributable to the job-side penalty, not to the candidate-side
penalty. These reductions reflect the intended behaviour: the market-maker redirects some users away from
their highest-scoring jobs toward less popular alternatives, accepting
a modest match quality cost in exchange for a substantially more
equitable distribution of both applications and recruiter attention.

On the candidate side, the full system achieves a Gini of 0.4950 with
only 3.9\% of candidates starved and 82.9\% in the safe zone —
improvements of 28.2\% and 21.8\% respectively over the
bilateral baseline. The addition of the candidate-side penalty over
universe~2 reduces the starved fraction further from 6.8\% to 3.9\%
and brings the congested fraction down marginally from 13.7\% to
13.2\%, confirming that the candidate penalty is doing its job but
cannot fully counteract the structural effect of small inboxes created
by the job penalty. The remaining congestion is an inherent tension in
the dual-sided design: the mechanisms that most effectively reduce
candidate starvation also create pathways for mild candidate congestion
that the candidate penalty can only partially suppress.

Figure~\ref{fig:hybrid_job_grid} and Figure~\ref{fig:hybrid_cand_grid}
visualise the distributions across all four universes. The job grid
confirms the paired structure: bilateral and candidate-sigmoid are
visually identical, as are job-sigmoid and both-sigmoid. The contrast
between the two pairs is stark — the penalised universes show a sharp
drop in the tail beyond the congested threshold and a clear build-up of
mass in the safe zone. The candidate grid shows all four panels
diverging, with the progression from bilateral through to both-sigmoid
clearly visible in the shrinking starved spike at zero and one and the
extending but flattening tail.
