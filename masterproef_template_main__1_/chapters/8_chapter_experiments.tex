\chapter{Experiments and Results}
\label{chap:experiments}
This chapter describes the experimental evaluation of the systems explained in previous chapters. First, the shared experiment settings are described in Section~\ref{sec:exp_settings} which includes dataset statistics, how the temporal split is applied, and a summary of the hardware and software configurations which were used. Then, experiments are presented for the user-side job recommender system (Section~\ref{sec:exp_user}), the recruiter-side suitability model (Section~\ref{sec:exp_recruiter}), and the congestion balancing strategies (Section~\ref{sec:exp_congestion}).

\section{General Experiments Setting}
\label{sec:exp_settings}
This section describes the general experimental settings that apply across all experiments in this chapter. This includes a description of the dataset, how the temporal window split is applied, and a summary of the hardware and software configuration used for all experiments. 
\subsection{CareerBuilder Dataset}
\label{sec:exp_dataset}

All experiments are conducted on the CareerBuilder Job Recommendation 
dataset~\cite{careerbuilderdataset}, consisting of real-world job 
applications, user profiles and job postings from CareerBuilder.com. 
The dataset spans 13 weeks and is organised into seven consecutive 
13-day temporal windows; each user and each job is assigned to exactly 
one window, and applications only occur between users and jobs active 
in the same window. Table~\ref{tab:dataset_stats} summarises the scale 
of the full dataset.

\begin{table}[htbp]
    \centering
    \caption{CareerBuilder dataset statistics.}
    \label{tab:dataset_stats}
    \begin{tabular}{lr}
        \toprule
        \textbf{Statistic} & \textbf{Value} \\
        \midrule
        Total unique users       & 389,708 \\
        Total unique jobs        & 1,091,923 \\
        Total applications       & 1,603,111 \\
        Number of windows        & 7 (13 days each) \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

Application behaviour is consistent across all seven windows: the 
median user submits 2 applications per window. The mean (4.5--5.6 depending on the window) substantially exceeds the median in every window, reflecting a 
heavy-tailed distribution in which a small number of highly active 
users account for a disproportionate share of total interactions. Because application behaviour is consistent across windows, the temporal split described in the next section is unlikely to introduce distributional shifts between the historical and deployment periods.

In each window, users in the original dataset are labeled as \texttt{Split=Train} or \texttt{Split=Test}. Users marked \texttt{Split=Test} are excluded 
entirely (22,838 users) including their applications (50,991). These users made five or more applications 
during the withheld 4-day test period of each window. Of these, 43\% 
have no recorded interactions in the 9-day training period whatsoever, 
meaning their only observable activity falls within the withheld 
portion. Including them would introduce users with incomplete or 
entirely missing interaction records into the evaluation, so they 
are excluded entirely.

Rather than using the original within-window train/test labels provided 
by the dataset, a temporal split across windows is applied, as 
illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:custom_temporal_split}. Window~7 
represents the target deployment period --- the future time window that 
all models in this thesis are designed to serve. No model is trained on Window~7 data; it is reserved as the sanity check and production 
window for all models. Windows~1--6 
provide the historical data from which each component learns and is 
developed, though the exact partitioning differs by component and is
described in the respective experiment sections.

An important consequence of this temporal split is that all users in Window~7 are cold-start users with no recorded interactions in the training period \cite{nadimi2014coldstartproblem}. This is a realistic reflection of the production setting, where every user at inference time is new to the model. This makes the evaluation more challenging but also more meaningful. \subsection{Hardware and Software Configuration}
\label{sec:hardware}

All experiments are run on the Artificial Intelligence and Data Analytics Research Group (AIDA) high-performance computing cluster via SSH. Training is performed on a single NVIDIA A40 GPU (46~GB VRAM). The software stack uses Python with PyTorch as the deep learning framework and scikit-learn for the lightweight recruiter-side models.

\section{User-Side Job Recommender System Experiments}
\label{sec:exp_user}
% ============================================================
The goal of the user-side experiments is to train and evaluate a 
\gls{TT} model that can rank relevant jobs highly for cold-start users 
--- users with no interaction history in the training period.  An ablation study is conducted to assess the impact of different model architectures and features. The practical 
utility of the model is additionally assessed qualitatively at inference 
time by inspecting recommendations for a sample of Window~7 users against 
the full Window~7 job pool.

\subsection{Experiment Setting}
\label{sec:exp_user_setting}

Windows~1--4 are used for training, Windows~5--6 for validation, and 
Window~7 serves as the qualitative evaluation window, consistent with 
the temporal split described in Section~\ref{sec:custom_split}.
\subsection{Dataset Creation}
\label{sec:exp_user_datagen}
A random sample of 25,000 users per window is drawn from Windows~1--4, 
creating a training set of 100,000 users and 461,473 positive pairs. 
Windows~5--6 are used in full for validation (78,428 users, 367,038 
positive pairs). For each positive pair $(u,j^+)$, four negatives are sampled according 
to a 2:1:1 ratio:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{2 cross-state negatives}: jobs from states other than the user's own state, sampled from the same temporal window. These teach the model that geographic fit is a genuine signal.
    \item \textbf{1 same-state negative}: a job from the user's state but a different city, active in the same window. This forces the model to look beyond state-level location and discriminate on career content.
    \item \textbf{1 same-city negative}: a job posted in the user's city but one the user did not apply to. These are the hardest negatives, as they share the user's location and are still unapplied to, pressuring the model to learn semantic fit.
\end{itemize}
 
The 2:1:1 ratio reflects the geographic structure of the real inference 
setting: when ranking a user against the full job pool, the vast majority 
of candidates are in other states, a smaller fraction share the user's 
state, and only a handful are in the same city. The effect of 
this is confirmed qualitatively in Section~\ref{sec:exp_user_inference}.

\subsection{Training Configuration and Hyperparameters}
\label{sec:exp_user_hyperparameters}

Each categorical feature is mapped to an embedding of dimension $d_{\text{cat}}$ = 16 (see Chapter~\ref{chap:user_recommendation}) and 
the text embedding is truncated to $d_{\text{text}} = 100$. 
Table~\ref{tab:tower_input_dims} shows the resulting input dimension 
breakdown: text accounts for approximately 50\% of the user tower 
input and 76\% of the job tower input, large enough to carry the 
semantic signal without suppressing the structured features.

Both towers share the same MLP structure: two hidden layers of 512 and 
256 units, followed by the $d$-dimensional output layer, giving a 
202→512→256→64 reduction for the user tower and 132→512→256→64 for 
the job tower. Batch normalisation and ReLU are applied after each 
hidden layer, with dropout rates of 0.3 and 0.2 for the first and 
second hidden layer respectively. The output dimension is $d = 64$.

The model is trained for a maximum of 50 epochs with early stopping 
(patience~= 5) based on validation loss, using the Adam optimiser 
with a learning rate of $10^{-3}$ and a batch size of 1024.

\subsection{Ablation Study}
\label{sec:exp_user_ablation}
 
To determine the optimal model configuration, a systematic ablation study is conducted comparing two design choices: whether to include location features (city and state) and whether to apply L2 normalisation to the output embeddings. All configurations are trained with the 2:1:1 negative sampling strategy described in Section~\ref{sec:dataset_construction} and use the same training settings described in Section~\ref{sec:exp_user_hyperparameters}. Table~\ref{tab:ablation_user} reports the results at the best validation checkpoint for each configuration.
 
It's important to note that Hit@K, Recall@K and NDCG@K are computed against the validation set's balanced candidate pool (positive and sampled negative jobs per user) rather than the full job catalogue. For example if a user has applied to 2 jobs, the metrics are computed against a pool of 2 positive jobs and 8 sampled negative jobs using the negative sampling strategy. As discussed in Section~\ref{sec:prelim_metrics}, this makes their absolute values more optimistic than what would be observed at full-scale inference. \gls{AUC-ROC} is therefore used as the primary comparison criterion, as it is less sensitive to this pool-size effect. 
 
\begin{table}[htbp]
    \centering
    \caption{Ablation study results at best validation checkpoint, early stopping (patience~= 5).}
    \label{tab:ablation_user}
    \begin{tabular}{llcccc}
        \toprule
        \textbf{Location Features} & \textbf{L2 Norm.} & \textbf{Val Loss} & \textbf{Accuracy} & \textbf{AUC} & \textbf{Epochs} \\
        \midrule
        Included & No  & 0.2706 & 88.90\% & \textbf{0.9242} & 19 \\
        Excluded & No  & 0.3463 & 84.92\% & 0.8641 &  9 \\
        Included & Yes & 0.4852 & 82.15\% & 0.7331 &  8 \\
        Excluded & Yes & 0.4892 & 81.73\% & 0.7426 &  4 \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}
 
Two findings emerge from the results. First, \textbf{L2 normalisation consistently hurts performance} regardless 
of whether location features are present — AUC drops from 0.9242 to 0.7331 
with location included, and from 0.8641 to 0.7426 without. This is an interesting finding considering that cosine similarity is a common practice to calculate embedding similarity \cite{you2025semanticsanglecosinesimilarity}. Normalising output embeddings to unit norm removes their magnitude, which prevents the model from encoding match confidence in the scale of the dot product. In this case, the model benefits from retaining this confidence information.
 
Second, \textbf{including location features improves performance substantially} — \gls{AUC-ROC} rises from 0.8641 to 0.9242. Including the location features allows the model to learn that cross-state negatives are less relevant than same-state negatives, and same-state negatives are less relevant than same-city negatives. Without these features, the model struggles to learn geographic preferences using only the job and user content features, which is a much harder signal to extract. In Section~\ref{sec:exp_user_inference} qualitative evidence is provided that the model has indeed learned a meaningful geographic signal, as the top-ranked recommendations for test users are geographically coherent with their location.
 
A complete table of all metrics for all four ablation configurations at their respective best validation checkpoints is provided in Appendix~\ref{app:ablation_full}.

output: whether recommended jobs are semantically consistent with the 
user's career history if available, and whether the locations of the recommended jobs are geographically coherent with the user's location.

This user is based in Deer Park, Texas, holds a high school diploma and has 18 years of work experience. Their last 3 jobs consists entirely of accounts payable and administrative finance roles: Accounts Payable Analyst, Administrative Assistant, and AP/AR support positions. All ten recommended jobs are in Houston and fall within the same financial-administrative cluster: Senior Accounts Payable Specialist, AP/AR Specialist, Accounting Assistant, Accounts Payable Clerk, and similar titles. This is a clean demonstration of the model working as intended: a clear career signal produces semantically consistent recommendations, and the location features ensure all recommendations are in the same geographic area as the user's location.

\section{Recruiter-Side Suitability Model Experiments}
\label{sec:exp_recruiter}
%============================================================
The goal of the recruiter-side experiments is to train and evaluate a suitability model that can rank candidates by their fit to a job, using the engineered feature set described in Section~\ref{sec:recruiter_features} and the LLM-generated labels described in Section~\ref{sec:recruiter_data_gen}. A two-stage model selection process is used to first identify the best-performing classifier type among logistic regression, random forest and XGBoost, and then to compare the best classifier across three different negative sampling strategies. Finally, the model is run at inference time to inspect the top-ranked candidates for a sample of Window~7 jobs and confirm that the model has learned meaningful signals of suitability.

\subsection{Experiment Setting}
\label{sec:exp_recruiter_setting}

LLM-labelled pairs are drawn from Windows~1--4. Unlike the user-side 
experiments, no window-level validation split is used: the labelled 
dataset is small (5{,}896 pairs) and a job-level split is sufficient 
for reliable model selection at this scale. Jobs are randomly 
partitioned into an 80\% pool for cross-validation and a held-out 
test set of 20\% used only once after the final model has been 
chosen. \gls{AUC-ROC} is used as the primary selection 
criterion. Window~7 is reserved for the qualitative inference evaluation.

\subsection{Dataset Creation}
\label{sec:exp_recruiter_datagen}

Labels are generated using the Qwen3.5 (35B) model served locally 
via the Ollama API, following the procedure described in 
Section~\ref{sec:recruiter_data_gen}. For each job, up to 20 applicants are evaluated, to ensure diverse jobs in the dataset. The resulting dataset contains 5,896 labeled pairs across 1,511 unique jobs, with the fit level distribution shown in Table~\ref{tab:recruiter_label_dist}. The full prompt 
and an example output are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:llm_prompt}.

\subsection{Stage 2: Negative Strategy Selection}
\label{sec:exp_recruiter_negatives}
 
Having established XGBoost as the strongest classifier, the model type is now held fixed and the negative construction strategy is varied. Table~\ref{tab:recruiter_neg_comparison} reports XGBoost test set performance for each strategy, where each model is evaluated on a test set constructed with the same negative type as its training data.
 
\subsubsection{Synthetic vs.\ LLM negatives}
The first interesting but expected result is the gap between the synthetic and LLM models: \gls{AUC} 0.852 vs.\ 0.724, a difference of 0.128. This gap is a task-difficulty effect. The synthetic model is tested on random non-applicants, who by construction are unlikely to match the job on any meaningful feature; almost any signal separates them from strong applicants. The LLM model is tested on poor-fit applicants from the real pool --- candidates who self-selected to apply despite a mismatch. These share the same self-selection signal as positives, forcing the model to rely on deeper feature differences. The lower \gls{AUC} correctly reflects the genuine difficulty of that discrimination task.
 
\subsubsection{Combined as the Best Choice}
The combined model achieves \gls{AUC} 0.755 on a mixed test set with equal proportions of LLM and synthetic negatives, outperforming the LLM-only model (0.724) but underperforming the synthetic-only model (0.852). Crucially, in the congestion balancing context of Chapter~\ref{chap:congestion_balancing}, the model must score candidates who may not have applied to the job at all --- which is precisely the synthetic scenario. A model trained exclusively on LLM negatives has never seen that setting and may not generalise well to it. The combined model handles both regimes and achieves the best verified performance across them.

\section{Congestion Mitigation Experiments} 
\label{sec:exp_congestion}

 
\subsection{Experiment Goal}
\label{sec:exp_congestion_goal}
 
The congestion balancing experiments pursue four goals in sequence,
each corresponding to one of the strategies developed in
Chapter~\ref{chap:congestion_balancing}. The first is to establish
that both forms of congestion are genuinely present in the data before
any intervention is applied, forming the baseline for all subsequent
comparisons. The second is to show that the job-side market-maker
independently reduces job congestion while preserving acceptable match
quality. The third is to show the same for the candidate-side
market-maker in isolation. The fourth is to demonstrate that bilateral
scoring combined with both market-maker corrections simultaneously
addresses congestion on both sides of the market.
\subsection{Experiment Setting}
\label{sec:exp_congestion_setting}
\paragraph{Simulation arena.}
The congestion balancing experiments operate on Window~7 (the deployment window introduced in Section~\ref{sec:custom_split}). Both the Two‑Tower and recruiter models are applied without additional fine‑tuning; market‑maker parameters are determined from Windows~1--6 and the resulting strategies are evaluated on Window~7 as a held‑out production simulation.

 
\paragraph{Parameter values.}
Tables~\ref{tab:job_mm_params} and~\ref{tab:cand_mm_params} list the
market-maker parameter values used in all congestion experiments. As with the
thresholds, these are design choices rather than optimised hyperparameters:
the boost and penalty magnitudes are set to be on the same $[0,1]$ probability
scale as the base scores they modify. Because both sides share the same lower
threshold $\tau_{\min} = 2$, the starved zone spans an identical range of two
units on both sides, and the boost parameters ($\gamma_b$, $\kappa_b$, $k_b$)
are consequently the same for both the job-side and candidate-side
market-makers. The penalty shape parameters ($\gamma_p$, $\kappa_p$, $k_p$)
differ between the two sides because the congested zones have different ranges:
the job-side hard cap is $H^{\text{job}}=50$, giving a penalty range of 40
units beyond $\tau_{\max}^{\text{job}}=9$, whereas the candidate-side hard cap
is $H^{\text{cand}}=10$, giving a penalty range of only 3 units beyond
$\tau_{\max}^{\text{cand}}=6$. In both cases the penalty parameters are
calibrated so that the smooth penalty reaches $P=-0.25$ at $H-1$ (i.e.\
49 applications for the job side, 9 shortlists for the candidate side).
At $H$ itself, a penalty of $-999.0$ is applied, effectively
removing the entity from consideration entirely.

Figures~\ref{fig:modification_functions} and~\ref{fig:modification_functions_cand}
illustrate the three functional forms for the job side and candidate side
respectively. On the job side, where the congested zone spans 40 units, the
three curves diverge clearly: the sigmoid descends steeply and saturates near
its maximum penalty well before the hard cap, making it the most aggressive
redistributor for moderately congested jobs; the quadratic starts slowly but
accelerates as congestion grows; and the linear descends at a constant rate,
reaching its maximum only at $H^{\text{job}}-1 = 49$. On the candidate side,
the compressed range of 3 units causes all three curves to converge rapidly
to $-0.25$, leaving little practical difference between them beyond the speed
of initial descent.

\paragraph{Difference between analyses.} One further distinction applies within the congestion experiments

applications generated by the \gls{TT} recommender, since the 
market-maker intervenes in the recommendation step itself. The

from Window~7, isolating the recruiter re-ranking mechanism. \subsubsection{Market-maker Simulation Results}
\label{sec:job_mm_results}
Table~\ref{tab:job_mm_results} compares all strategies on Window~7. The first
notable finding is that the free-market \gls{TT} recommender makes congestion
\emph{worse} than the historical baseline: the Gini rises from 0.8575 to

expected as the \gls{TT} model optimises for relevance and will confidently
direct many users towards the same popular jobs, amplifying the feedback loop
rather than correcting it. This result motivates the market-maker intervention
directly.

All three market-maker strategies produce a large improvement over the free
market. The Gini drops from 0.9625 to between 0.5741 and 0.5994 depending on
the strategy, and the safe zone grows from 6.4\% to between 23.8\% and 46.2\%.

sigmoid strategy achieves the best overall balance: it has the lowest Gini
(0.5741), the smallest starved fraction (51.3\%), and the largest safe zone

fraction (63.4\%), outperforming the quadratic on both metrics. The quadratic
strategy has the highest Gini (0.5994) and the largest starved fraction
(74.2\%).

These differences follow directly from the shape of each penalty function (Figure~\ref{fig:modification_functions}) and are clearly visible in the application distributions (Figure~\ref{fig:all_universes_job_congestion}). The free-market panel confirms the inequity: a massive spike at zero and a heavy tail extending well beyond 50 applications, with 623 jobs exceeding this threshold. All three market-maker strategies successfully suppress this extreme tail, though a large zero spike persists across all panels, reflecting jobs that received no recommendations regardless of strategy. The quadratic strategy produces the longest tail because its penalty is lenient near $\tau_{\max}$ and only accelerates later, allowing traffic to bleed through gradually into the congested zone. The linear strategy produces a shorter tail by descending at a constant rate, while the sigmoid produces the smallest tail due to its steep descent and early saturation of the penalty. A further consequence of this steepness is visible in the distributions as a local maximum just beyond $\tau_{\max}=9$: the faster the penalty ramps up beyond the threshold, the more sharply it discourages further applications, causing jobs to accumulate just past the boundary. This effect is most visible under the sigmoid, but nearly absent under the quadratic. The same logic applies to the starved zone. Finally, the congested fraction remains low and stable across all strategies (2.1--2.4\%), confirming that the market-maker genuinely redistributes traffic toward the safe zone rather than simply pushing it from congested into the void.

The match quality cost is modest across all strategies. The average \gls{TT}
probability $\bar{p}_{\text{TT}}$ drops from 0.9537 under free market to between 0.8837 and 0.8963
--- a reduction of 6--7\%. The average applied recruiter probability $\bar{p}_{\text{rec}}^{\text{app}}$ also decreases
slightly (0.4976 free market vs 0.4737--0.4788 with penalties), a reduction of
around 4\%. This reduction is expected as the market-maker intentionally promotes less popular jobs and doesn't optimise for relevance directly.

recommendations, the same two users from Section~\ref{sec:exp_recruiter_sanity}

summarised in Tables~\ref{tab:user668904_profile} and~\ref{tab:user831532_profile}.
Full top-10 recommendation lists are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:sanity_check_full};

were already discussed in Section~\ref{sec:exp_user_inference}.

\textbf{User 668904 — clear career signal.} The market-maker strategies
broaden the free-market list while preserving semantic coherence. Under
linear and sigmoid, the top recommendations shift toward Tax Accountant,
Financial Accountant, Senior Accountant, and Accounting Analyst roles ---
all squarely within the user's finance domain and with high original
probabilities (0.95--0.98). The sigmoid surfaces more senior and specialised

cluster more aggressively. Under quadratic, Document Controller~4 and Energy
Quantitative Analyst occupy ranks~1--2 with lower original probabilities
(0.848 and 0.817) --- both had 0~prior applications at ranking time, earning
them the maximum possible boost under the quadratic penalty. Overall, all
three strategies successfully diversify the recommendation list without
sacrificing domain relevance.

\textbf{User 831532 — no job history.} The market-maker strategies maintain
geographic coherence across the PA/NJ/DE tri-state area. Under all three
strategies, the core care cluster (Shelter Staff, Youth Care Workers,

introducing some semantic noise for this care-adjacent profile. The sigmoid

coherent Pharmacy Technician and Certified Home Health Aides at ranks~1--2.
Under quadratic, ranks~2 and~3 are occupied by jobs with 21 and 25
applications --- where the quadratic
penalty is modest, allowing their high original probabilities (0.979

The contrast between the two users illustrates the fundamental trade-off:
when the base model is confident and the candidate pool is rich, the
market-maker redirects traffic toward equally relevant alternatives with
minimal quality cost. When the base model has little signal to work with,
boosted low-application jobs can displace coherent recommendations. This
is expected behaviour --- the market-maker is designed to optimise
distributional fairness at the market level, not to improve individual
recommendation quality.

\subsection{Bilateral Scoring}
\label{sec:exp_bilateral}
% ============================================================
 
This section and the next use the sigmoid market-maker, since it produced the
best results in the standalone job-side experiments
(Section~\ref{sec:exp_job_congestion}) and performed on par with the
others on the candidate side (Section~\ref{sec:exp_user_congestion}). It's important to note the distinction between the standalone market-maker experiments and the bilateral/hybrid experiments mentioned in Section~\ref{sec:exp_congestion_setting}. 
 
\paragraph{Job-side.}
Table~\ref{tab:hybrid_job} shows that bilateral scoring alone already
improves over the pure \gls{TT} free market: the job-side Gini drops
from 0.9625 to 0.9182 and the starved fraction decreases from 90.8\%
to 85.6\%, with no explicit congestion penalty applied. This
improvement arises because the \gls{TT} model was optimised purely for
user-side relevance, without accounting for whether the user is
actually a competitive candidate for the role. By weighting the
recruiter signal equally alongside the \gls{TT} score, the combined
ranking steers users toward jobs where they are both interested and
genuinely suitable, diversifying which jobs receive traffic and
partially breaking the feedback loop that concentrates applications on
popular postings.
 
\paragraph{Candidate-side.}
On the candidate side (Table~\ref{tab:hybrid_candidate}), the TT-only
free market produces a Gini of 0.7896 with 79.7\% of candidates
starved, confirming that an uncorrected recommender generates highly
unequal shortlisting distributions on the candidate side as well.
Bilateral scoring substantially reduces this: the Gini falls to 0.6326
with 32.1\% of candidates starved. The improvement arises for the same
reason as on the job side --- steering users toward jobs where they are
genuinely competitive produces smaller, more selective inboxes and
naturally spreads recruiter attention more evenly across candidates.

\paragraph{Match quality.}
The average \gls{TT} probability $p_{\text{TT}}$ drops from 0.9537 to 0.8966 --- a reduction 
of 5.9\% --- while $\bar{p}_{\text{rec}}^{\text{app}}$ rises substantially 
from 0.4976 to 0.8405, confirming that the combined score steers users toward 
jobs where they are genuinely competitive. The average shortlisted recruiter 
probability $\bar{p}_{\text{rec}}^{\text{short}}$ is 0.8666, indicating that 
the candidates surfaced to recruiters are genuinely suitable despite the more 
selective inbox composition.

\paragraph{Job-side.}
Table~\ref{tab:hybrid_job} shows the job-side Gini falling from
0.9182 to 0.7130, with the starved fraction dropping from 85.6\% to
63.3\% and the safe zone growing from 11.1\% to 34.0\%. This
improvement is smaller than in the standalone job-side experiment,
where the same sigmoid penalty achieved a Gini of 0.5741. The
difference is explained by the structure of the combined scoring
function (Equation~\ref{eq:combined_score}): the job penalty
$\delta_\text{job}$ acts only on the Two-Tower component, which
carries weight $\alpha = 0.5$, meaning the penalty is effectively
halved in the combined score. The recruiter probability term is
congestion-unaware and pulls the combined score in the opposite
direction. This is a fundamental consequence of the bilateral design
and represents a tunable trade-off: a higher $\alpha$ would strengthen
the penalty but reduce recruiter influence.
 
\paragraph{Candidate-side.}
On the candidate side (Table~\ref{tab:hybrid_candidate}), the full

fraction rises to 13.5\% due to the dual-sided design: the job-side
penalty redistributes applications toward previously empty jobs,
creating small inboxes where all applicants are automatically
shortlisted, including candidates already frequently shortlisted
elsewhere. The candidate-side penalty suppresses this effect but
cannot fully counteract it, leading to a modest increase in
congestion.
 
\paragraph{Match quality.}
The average \gls{TT} probability $\bar{p}_\text{TT}$ is 0.8645 and 
$\bar{p}_{\text{rec}}^{\text{app}}$ is 0.8106 --- both lower than the 
bilateral baseline, as expected. The average shortlisted recruiter probability 
$\bar{p}_{\text{rec}}^{\text{short}}$ drops from 0.8666 to 0.8133, a reduction 
of 6.3\% over the bilateral baseline which is expected because of the market-maker intervention. However, both recruiter probabilities 
remain well above the TT-only free market ($\bar{p}_{\text{rec}}^{\text{app}}$ 
= 0.4976), confirming that the bilateral scoring gain in candidate-job fit is 
preserved even after both penalties are applied.
