\chapter{Experiments}
\label{chap:experiments}
This chapter describes the experimental evaluation of the systems developed in previous chapters. First, the shared experiment settings are described in Section~\ref{sec:exp_settings} which includes the custom dataset split, dataset statistics, and hardware configuration. Then, experiments are presented for the user-side recommendation system (Section~\ref{sec:exp_user}), the recruiter-side recommendation system (Section~\ref{sec:exp_recruiter}), and the congestion balancing strategies (Section~\ref{sec:exp_congestion}).

\section{Experiment Settings}
\label{sec:exp_settings}

\subsection{Dataset}
\label{sec:exp_dataset}
All experiments are conducted on the CareerBuilder Job Recommendation dataset~\cite{careerbuilderdataset}, consisting of real-world job applications, user profiles and job postings from CareerBuilder.com over a 13-week period. The dataset is organised into seven consecutive 13-day temporal windows where users apply to jobs which are active in the same window. Table~\ref{tab:dataset_stats} summarises the scale of the full dataset.

\begin{table}[htbp]
    \centering
    \caption{CareerBuilder dataset statistics.}
    \label{tab:dataset_stats}
    \begin{tabular}{lr}
        \toprule
        \textbf{Statistic} & \textbf{Value} \\
        \midrule
        Total unique users       & 389,708 \\
        Total unique jobs        & 1,091,923 \\
        Total applications       & 1,603,111 \\
        Interaction matrix sparsity & 99.9996\% \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

The extremely high sparsity — fewer than one in 200,000 user-job pairs has a recorded interaction — means thatcollaborative approaches relying on interaction density are infeasible. The two-tower architecture instead uses interactions as a training signal while generalising through user and job profile features, making it robust to sparse data.

Rather than using the original within-window train/test labels provided by the dataset, a temporal split across windows is applied (Figure~\ref{fig:custom_temporal_split}). Windows 1--4 form the training set and Windows 5--7 form the validation and test set, with Windows 5--6 used for validation and Window 7 reserved for final evaluation.

Users marked \texttt{Split=Test} are excluded entirely (22,838 users). These users made five or more applications during the withheld 4-day test period of the original competition. Of these, 43\% have no recorded interactions in the training period whatsoever, as their only observable activity falls within the withheld test period. Including them would introduce users with structurally missing interaction records into the evaluation set, so they are excluded entirely.

This design has three consequences. First, there is a strict temporal boundary: the model never sees any interaction from the evaluation period during training. Second, the evaluation is a genuine cold-start scenario — no user appears in both the training and evaluation sets. Third, using all active users from each window group (rather than filtering by activity thresholds) avoids biasing the evaluation towards highly-engaged users. Table~\ref{tab:split_stats} shows the resulting split sizes.

\subsection{Hardware and Software Configuration}
\label{sec:hardware}

All experiments are run on the Artificial Intelligence and Data Analytics Research Group (AIDA) high-performance computing cluster via SSH. Training is performed on a single NVIDIA A40 GPU (46~GB VRAM). The software stack uses Python with PyTorch as the deep learning framework, scikit-learn for the lightweight recruiter-side models, and the Sentence Transformers library for Qwen3 text embeddings.

\section{User-Side Recommendation Experiments}
\label{sec:exp_user}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Experiment Goal}
\label{sec:exp_user_goal}

The goal of the user-side experiments is to train and evaluate a two-tower model that can rank relevant jobs highly for cold-start users — users with no interaction history in the training period. The model is trained on Windows 1--4, validated on Windows 5--6, and evaluated on Window 7, where it must generalise to entirely new users and jobs. Success is measured primarily by \gls{ROC-AUC} during training, as this metric is least sensitive to the candidate pool size of the validation set. The practical utility of the model is assessed qualitatively at inference time by inspecting recommendations for a sample of test users against the full Window 7 job pool.

\subsection{Experiment Setting}
\label{sec:exp_user_setting}

Due to the large number of training users (189,657), a random subsample of 100,000 users is drawn from Windows 1--4 to keep training computationally feasible, while all validation and test users are retained in full. The model is trained using the Adam optimiser with a learning rate of $10^{-3}$, a batch size of 1024, and an output embedding dimension of $d=64$. Early stopping is applied with a patience of 5 epochs based on validation loss, with a maximum of 50 epochs. The categorical embedding dimension is set to 16 per feature, and the text embedding dimension is set to $d_{\text{text}}=100$, yielding a total job tower input of 132 dimensions — a balance that ensures the text signal is significant but not dominant.

\subsubsection{Training Data Generation}
\label{sec:training_data_gen}

Positive samples are extracted directly from the application records: each application constitutes a confirmed user-job match with label $y=1$. Negative samples are generated synthetically using a city-based strategy at a 4:1 ratio — four negative job samples per positive application. This ratio approximates the real inference distribution while remaining computationally tractable; a much higher ratio (e.g.\ 100:1) would more closely mirror inference but would make training infeasible at this dataset scale.

\paragraph{City-based negative sampling.}
\label{sec:negative_sampling}
For each user-window pair, negative jobs are sampled preferentially from the same city as the user, as these represent realistic alternatives the user might genuinely consider. If insufficient same-city jobs exist in the window, jobs from other cities serve as fallback negatives. By drawing negatives from the same city, location is deliberately removed as a discriminative signal during training — both positive and negative jobs share the same geographic context. Semantic job-fit based on career history, education and job title similarity is therefore the primary signal the model must learn. Geographic preference is applied separately as a post-processing step at inference time, as described in Section~\ref{sec:inference_postprocessing}.

\paragraph{Evaluation caveat.}
Validation metrics reported during training are computed over the balanced validation set (4:1 negative ratio), yielding a candidate pool of roughly five jobs per user. This is far smaller than the full Window~7 job pool used at inference, so ranking metrics such as Hit@$K$ and NDCG@$K$ are optimistic relative to real-world performance. \gls{ROC-AUC} is less sensitive to this effect and is therefore used as the primary training signal. A qualitative evaluation against the full job pool is reported in Section~\ref{sec:exp_user_inference}.

\subsection{Ablation Study}
\label{sec:exp_user_ablation}

To determine the optimal model configuration, a systematic ablation study is conducted comparing two design choices: whether to include location features (city and state) and whether to apply L2 normalisation to the output embeddings. Each configuration is trained for 3 epochs under identical conditions and compared by validation \gls{ROC-AUC}. Table~\ref{tab:ablation_user} reports the results.

\begin{table}[htbp]
    \centering
    \caption{Ablation study results after 3 training epochs (validation \gls{ROC-AUC}).}
    \label{tab:ablation_user}
    \begin{tabular}{llccc}
        \toprule
        \textbf{Location Features} & \textbf{L2 Norm.} & \textbf{Val Loss} & \textbf{Accuracy} & \textbf{AUC} \\
        \midrule
        Excluded & No  & 0.3513 & 84.60\% & 0.8591 \\
        Excluded & Yes & 0.4687 & 84.18\% & 0.7954 \\
        Included & No  & 0.2317 & 91.24\% & \textbf{0.9365} \\
        Included & Yes & 0.4693 & 84.13\% & 0.7950 \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

Two findings emerge. First, L2 normalisation consistently hurts performance regardless of whether location is included. Without normalisation, the dot product score is unbounded, allowing the model to encode match confidence in both the direction and magnitude of the embedding vectors. Normalising to unit norm discards the magnitude, reducing the model's representational capacity.

Second, including location features with a shared vocabulary yields a substantial improvement — \gls{ROC-AUC} rises from 0.8591 to 0.9365. This is at first surprising, since city-based negative sampling should prevent location from being learned as a direct discriminative signal. The improvement is better explained by the additional model capacity: the city and state embedding layers allow the model to capture latent associations between location and job market characteristics, such as industries that cluster in particular cities. The importance of the shared vocabulary is confirmed by the fact that including location features without it — in an earlier experimental phase — actually hurt performance, since independently encoded integers referred to different cities in the two towers and introduced noise rather than signal.

Based on these results, the final model uses location features with no L2 normalisation.

\subsection{Final Model Results}
\label{sec:user_results}

The final model is trained with the optimal configuration for a maximum of 50 epochs with early stopping (patience = 5). Training converges at epoch 11, at which point the best model checkpoint — saved at the epoch of lowest validation loss — achieves the results shown in Table~\ref{tab:final_results_user}.

\begin{table}[htbp]
    \centering
    \caption{Final model results on the validation set (best checkpoint).}
    \label{tab:final_results_user}
    \begin{tabular}{lr}
        \toprule
        \textbf{Metric} & \textbf{Value} \\
        \midrule
        Training Loss   & 0.1731 \\
        Validation Loss & 0.2254 \\
        Accuracy        & 91.76\% \\
        AUC-ROC         & 0.9456 \\
        \midrule
        Hit@5           & 0.9962 \\
        Recall@5        & 0.8479 \\
        NDCG@5          & 0.9200 \\
        Hit@10          & 0.9995 \\
        Recall@10       & 0.9320 \\
        NDCG@10         & 0.9294 \\
        Hit@20          & 0.9999 \\
        Recall@20       & 0.9740 \\
        NDCG@20         & 0.9368 \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

The AUC-ROC of 0.9456 indicates strong discriminative ability — the model ranks a randomly chosen applied job above a randomly chosen non-applied job 94.56\% of the time. Early convergence at epoch 11 is consistent with the regularisation applied throughout both towers (batch normalisation and dropout), which prevents overfitting without requiring many epochs.

The ranking metrics (Hit@$K$, Recall@$K$, NDCG@$K$) are high due to the small candidate pool used during validation (4:1 negative ratio), as discussed in Section~\ref{sec:negative_sampling}. They should not be interpreted as reflecting real-world retrieval performance; the qualitative evaluation in Section~\ref{sec:exp_user_inference} provides a more realistic assessment.
