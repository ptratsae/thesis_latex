The job market is a two-sided platform where jobseekers and recruiters
have distinct and often competing preferences. A structural problem in
such markets is congestion: when a recommender system surfaces the same
popular jobs to many users, applications concentrate on a small subset
of postings. This leaves the majority of jobs starved of candidates
while a small number is overwhelmed. The concentration of applications on
popular jobs has a compounding effect on the candidate side: when many
jobseekers compete for the same positions, only a small number of
candidates are ever seen by recruiters, while the rest are ignored. Recommender systems that optimise purely for relevance
amplify this dynamic, since popularity bias causes them to
systematically favour already-visible jobs, creating a feedback loop that worsens congestion and reduces overall market efficiency. This dissertation addresses this imbalance by proposing a dual-phase
AI-driven recommendation framework for two-sided job markets that
reduces congestion while preserving fair and efficient matches. On the
user side, a Two-Tower neural architecture generates personalised job
recommendation lists for jobseekers; on the recruiter side, a
lightweight XGBoost model scores candidate suitability for
shortlisting. Because recruiter training labels were unavailable, a
large language model is used to synthesise them, producing a compact
model suited for fast inference. Both components are integrated into a
hybrid congestion-balancing strategy that applies job-level score
adjustments using market-makers and the scores of both the user-side and recruiter-side models at recommendation time and candidate-level adjustments
during recruiter shortlisting. The approach is evaluated via
counterfactual simulations on real application logs from the
CareerBuilder dataset, using applications-per-job and
shortlists-per-candidate distributions, Gini coefficients, and
model-based match probabilities as metrics.
