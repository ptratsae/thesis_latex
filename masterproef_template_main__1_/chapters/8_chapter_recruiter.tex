\chapter{Recruiter-Side Suitability Model}
\label{chap:recruiter_recommendation}

This chapter describes the recruiter-side suitability model, which predicts the suitability of a candidate for a given job posting from a recruiter's perspective. It complements the job recommender system described in Chapter~\ref{chap:user_recommendation}: where the model learns which jobs a seeker is likely to apply to, the recruiter-side model learns which candidates are genuinely qualified for a role. The suitability score serves two purposes in Chapter~\ref{chap:congestion_balancing}: it acts as a second component in the bilateral match score, ensuring that recommended jobs are not only relevant to the seeker but also viable from the recruiter's perspective, and it enables candidate ranking by ordering a job's applicant pool by predicted fit. This way candidate-side congestion can also be measured and mitigated.

The chapter begins by motivating and describing the synthetic label generation pipeline (Section~\ref{sec:recruiter_data_gen}), since the absence of explicit recruiter feedback makes this a necessary foundation before any model can be trained. From there, the feature engineering is described in Section~\ref{sec:recruiter_features}, covering both the heuristic and semantic signals that form the input to the classifier. The chosen lightweight model is presented in Section~\ref{sec:recruiter_lightweight}.  Finally, Section~\ref{sec:recruiter_inference} describes how the trained model is applied at inference time. The performance of the model is evaluated in Section~\ref{sec:exp_recruiter}.

\section{Data Generation and Labeling}
\label{sec:recruiter_data_gen}

While job application datasets record which candidates applied to which 
jobs, they contain no information about the recruiter's evaluation of 
those candidates (see Section~\ref{sec:intro:problem}). 
 
To address this, an \gls{LLM} is used as a synthetic labeler. The \gls{LLM} is prompted using a \gls{CoT} approach (introduced in Section~\ref{sec:prelim_cot}) to evaluate the fit between a candidate profile and a job posting. Its output is then converted into binary labels. This approach is justified by recent evidence that \glspl{LLM}, when prompted with explicit criteria and structured reasoning, produce evaluations that correlate well with human judgment in recruitment contexts~\cite{wei2023chainofthoughtpromptingelicitsreasoning}.

\subsection{Synthetic Label Generation}
\label{sec:recruiter_llm}

For each $(u,j)$ pair, the \gls{LLM} is prompted to derive a role's must-haves, nice-to-haves, and deal-breakers directly from the job description, and then to evaluate the candidate profile against those criteria. Letting the criteria emerge from the posting itself, rather than from a fixed taxonomy, more closely mirrors how a recruiter reads a job description.

Based on this evaluation, the \gls{LLM} assigns each $(u,j)$ pair one of four 
fit levels: \textit{Very Good Fit}, \textit{Good Fit}, \textit{Poor Fit}, 
or \textit{Very Poor Fit}. The four-level scale requires the model to reason about 
\textit{degree} of fit rather than a simple binary judgment, 
producing more deliberate reasoning traces before the labels 
are collapsed to binary for training. These are then mapped to binary labels: $y = 1$ for 
the two positive classes (\textit{Very Good Fit} and \textit{Good Fit}) and $y = 0$ for the two negative classes (\textit{Poor Fit} and \textit{Very Poor Fit}).

\paragraph{Prompt design.}
The prompt was developed iteratively, with each revision guided by inspection of outputs on sampled $(u,j)$ pairs. The key insight driving the design is that without structured criteria, the \gls{LLM} applies inconsistent implicit thresholds across independent calls. Explicit \gls{CoT} instructions resolve this by forcing the model to 
reason through the same criteria for every $(u,j)$ pair before 
assigning a fit level. Explicit rules for each fit level ensure 
consistent thresholds, and extra notes address remaining inconsistencies 
observed during iterative refinement. The output is returned in JSON format, which enables sanity checking the reasoning behind the \gls{LLM} easily. The specific prompt and example output are given in the Appendix~\ref{app:llm_prompt}.

\subsection{Negative Sampling Strategies}
\label{sec:recruiter_balanced}


Three different datasets are constructed for training and evaluation. The first dataset uses only \gls{LLM}-generated labels: positives 
are $(u,j)$ pairs labeled \textit{Good Fit} or \textit{Very Good Fit}, 
and negatives are pairs labeled \textit{Poor Fit} or \textit{Very Poor Fit}. This distinction is the most challenging task, as the model needs to learn to distinguish good candidates from bad candidates among the pool of applicants.

The second dataset is constructed by using the same positives, but the negatives are randomly sampled from a set of $(u,j)$ pairs where the job history's words of a user do not overlap with the job's title to ensure fewer false negatives are included. This dataset is used to evaluate the model's ability to distinguish good candidates from the entire pool. This task is easier as the negatives are more likely to be truly unqualified.  

Finally, a combined dataset is constructed by sampling an equal number of negatives from the \gls{LLM}-generated negatives and the random negatives. This dataset is used to train a model that can generalise to both the within-applicant-pool ranking scenario and the broader candidate-scoring context that arises during congestion balancing in Chapter~\ref{chap:congestion_balancing}.

The choice of negative construction strategy and its effect on model 
behaviour are evaluated in Section~\ref{sec:exp_recruiter_negatives}, 
where the combined strategy is adopted as the final training configuration.

\section{Feature Engineering}
\label{sec:recruiter_features}

The feature vector $\mathbf{f}$ combines heuristic features derived from explicit domain rules --- such as experience gap or degree alignment --- with semantic features derived from neural embeddings. This hybrid design is motivated by the structure of the \gls{LLM} evaluation: the labeler considers both concrete, rule-based criteria (years of experience, degree requirements) and deeper semantic alignment (skill overlap, career trajectory). The features are designed to mirror these dimensions as much as possible so that the trained classifier can learn to replicate the labeler's reasoning on unseen $(u,j)$ pairs.

\subsection{Heuristic Features}
\label{sec:recruiter_heuristic}

Heuristic features encode recruitment domain knowledge in an 
interpretable form, with each feature corresponding to a concrete 
and explainable aspect of candidate-job fit.

\paragraph{Experience delta.} This feature captures the gap between the candidate's recorded experience and the experience required by the job:

\begin{equation}
    \delta_{\text{exp}} = \text{exp}_u - \text{exp}_j^{\text{req}}
\end{equation}

where $\text{exp}_u$ is the candidate's total years of experience and $\text{exp}_j^{\text{req}}$ is extracted from the job requirements text using a regex pattern that identifies phrases such as \textit{``5+ years of experience''} or \textit{``minimum 3 years required''}. When no requirement is stated or found, $\delta_{\text{exp}}$ is set to 0. A positive value indicates the candidate meets or exceeds the requirement; a negative value indicates a shortfall. This regex extraction is noisy and may fail, which is why the raw experience feature is also included as a backup signal.

\paragraph{TF-IDF keyword overlap.}
\gls{TF-IDF} represents a document as a vector of term weights, 
where each weight reflects how frequently a term appears in the 
document, scaled by how rarely it appears across the corpus --- 
rewarding distinctive, informative terms over common ones. A vectorizer is fit on all job postings in the training set; 
each candidate is represented by a document constructed from 
their job history and degree type. The cosine similarity between 
the two \gls{TF-IDF} vectors yields:

\begin{equation}
    \text{tfidf\_overlap} = \cos\!\left(\mathbf{v}_u^{\text{tfidf}},\, 
    \mathbf{v}_j^{\text{tfidf}}\right) \in [0, 1]
\end{equation}

This feature captures explicit keyword matches --- programming 
languages, domain terms, tool names --- that are often decisive 
in recruiter decisions and complements the embedding-based 
similarity.

\paragraph{Total years of experience.} The raw value $\text{exp}_u$ is included alongside $\delta_{\text{exp}}$, since the experience delta is uninformative when no requirement is stated or the regex extraction fails, and an absolute experience level remains a useful stand-alone signal in those cases. This feature is part of the dataset, meaning no regex extraction is needed which makes it more reliable than the experience delta feature. If the feature is missing, it is equal to 0.

\paragraph{Degree match.} This feature indicates whether the candidate's degree type matches any degree requirements stated in the job posting. The degree requirements are again extracted using regex patterns. More specifically, if the user has the degree needed or a higher degree, the feature is set to 1. If the user has no degree but the job requires one, the feature is set to -1. If the user has a degree but the job does not require one, the feature is set to 0.5. If neither the user has a degree nor the job requires one, the feature is set to 0. The same formatting limitations noted for $\delta_{\text{exp}}$ 
apply here. The semantic Profile Similarity feature described in Section~\ref{sec:recruiter_semantic} serves as a backup signal for degree fit when the regex fails to extract requirements.

\paragraph{Experience freshness.} This feature captures the ratio of the candidate's experience to the years since graduation, which serves as a proxy for how recent and relevant the experience is:
\begin{equation}
    \text{experience\_freshness} = \frac{\text{exp}_u}{\text{current\_year} - \text{graduation\_year} + 1}
\end{equation}
where $\text{current\_year} = 2026$ is fixed as the reference year 
for all pairs in the dataset. Table~\ref{tab:recruiter_heuristic_features} summarises the heuristic features used in the model.

\subsection{Semantic Features}
\label{sec:recruiter_semantic}

Semantic features are derived from the neural embeddings introduced in Chapter~\ref{chap:user_recommendation} and from an additional profile-level embeddings.

\paragraph{Cosine Similarity of Job Title and Job History.} Using the already computed Qwen3 embeddings from Chapter~\ref{chap:user_recommendation}, the cosine similarity between the $d_{\text{text}}$-dimensional embeddings of the candidate's job history and the job's title is computed directly:

\begin{equation}
    \text{cosine\_sim} = \frac{\mathbf{e}_u \cdot \mathbf{e}_j}{\|\mathbf{e}_u\| \cdot \|\mathbf{e}_j\|}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Interaction matrix via PCA.} To capture non-linear interactions 
between the two embeddings that a single cosine similarity score cannot 
express, an interaction matrix is constructed by concatenating the Hadamard 
product and the absolute element-wise difference:

\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{m}_{uj} = \left[\mathbf{e}_u \odot \mathbf{e}_j \;\Big|\; 
    |\mathbf{e}_u - \mathbf{e}_j|\right] \in \mathbb{R}^{2d_{\text{text}}}
\end{equation}

where $\odot$ denotes element-wise multiplication. The dot product captures 
feature-level interactions, while the absolute difference captures distance 
in the embedding space. Since $2d_{\text{text}}$ dimensions are 
high-dimensional and correlated, \gls{PCA} reduces the interaction vector 
to $d_{\text{pca}}$ components:

\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{p}_{uj} = \text{PCA}_{d_{\text{pca}}}(\mathbf{m}_{uj}) 
    \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{pca}}}
\end{equation}

This compression is justified on two grounds: the Hadamard product 
and absolute difference share significant covariance structure, making 
a large fraction of the $2d_{\text{text}}$ dimensions redundant; and 
reducing the feature space lowers the risk of XGBoost overfitting on 
the limited training data. The specific value of $d_{\text{pca}}$ and 
the variance retained are reported in Section~\ref{sec:exp_recruiter_training_config}.

\paragraph{Profile Similarity.} To capture profile-level fit beyond 
job-title embeddings, the remaining user profile information (degree, 
major, experience) and the full job description are encoded using 
MiniLM~\cite{minilm}, a lighter encoder chosen over Qwen3 for 
computational efficiency given the length of concatenation of title, requirements and description. The candidate and job information are concatenated into two documents:

\begin{align}
    \text{doc}_u &= \textit{``Degree: } d\textit{. Major: } m\textit{. Experience: } e\textit{ years.''} \\
    \text{doc}_j &= \textit{``Title: } t\textit{. Description: \ldots{} Requirements: \ldots''}
\end{align}

and the cosine similarity between embeddings is computed:

\begin{equation}
    \text{full\_profile\_cosine\_sim} = \cos\!\left(\text{enc}(\text{doc}_u),\, \text{enc}(\text{doc}_j)\right)
\end{equation}

This provides a complementary semantic signal to the cosine similarity of job title and job history embeddings. Table~\ref{tab:recruiter_semantic_features} summarises the semantic features.

\section{Lightweight Recruiter Architecture}
\label{sec:recruiter_lightweight}

The recruiter-side suitability model is a binary classifier that takes a feature vector $\mathbf{f} \in \mathbb{R}^{F}$ as input and outputs a suitability probability $\hat{y} \in [0,1]$. A lightweight architecture is chosen due to the fact that generating the dataset is time-consuming and the available data is limited. A more complex model, such as a \gls{TT} neural network, would be prone to overfitting and would not provide significant performance gains given the limited data.

\subsection{Model Selection}

Several classifiers are considered as candidates: Logistic Regression, Random Forest, XGBoost, and a soft-voting ensemble of all three. Each is evaluated under the same group-based cross-validation protocol to ensure a fair comparison. Section~\ref{sec:exp_recruiter_model_comparison} reports the full comparison; based on those results, XGBoost is selected as the final classifier and held fixed for all subsequent experiments. XGBoost is a gradient-boosted decision tree ensemble~\cite{Chen_2016} 
that builds an additive sequence of shallow trees, each correcting 
the residual errors of the previous one. 

The training protocol --- including data partitioning, 
cross-validation strategy, and leakage prevention --- is described 
in Section~\ref{sec:exp_recruiter_training_config}, where the 
specific hyperparameters and dataset sizes are also reported.





\section{Usage at Inference Time}
\label{sec:recruiter_inference}
% ==============================================================
 
At inference time the model is applied in two modes depending on the context. In the single-pair mode, a feature vector $\mathbf{f}$ is assembled for a specific $(u,j)$ pair, scaled with the fitted \texttt{StandardScaler}, and passed through the serialised XGBoost model to produce a suitability probability $\hat{y}$. This mode is used for qualitative inspection and sanity-checking.
 
In the batch mode, the model scores all pairs $(u,j)$ for a fixed job $j$ simultaneously. Because the job-side features (TF-IDF document, Qwen embedding, MiniLM embedding) are fixed across all candidates for a given job, they are computed once and reused. The candidate-side features are then extracted in a vectorised pass over all candidates, yielding a feature matrix $\mathbf{F} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times F}$ that is scaled and scored in a single call to the classifier. This produces a probability estimate $\hat{y}_i$ for each of the $N$ candidates, ranking them by predicted suitability without per-pair iteration overhead. This batch mode is the one used by the congestion balancing system in Chapter~\ref{chap:congestion_balancing}, where the recruiter score serves as the second component of the bilateral match score.
 
In Section~\ref{sec:exp_recruiter_sanity} examples of the model's output are provided for a sample of jobs from the deployment window, together with a qualitative discussion of whether the ranking of actual applicants aligns with expectations given their profiles.
