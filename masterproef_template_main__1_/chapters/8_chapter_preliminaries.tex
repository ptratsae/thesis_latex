\section{Recommender Systems Background}
\label{sec:prelim_rs_background}

This section introduces the concepts specific to recommender systems that underpin this work: a taxonomy of filtering approaches, and the two-sided market dynamics that shape the job recommendation problem.

\subsection{Recommender Systems}
\label{sec:prelim_rs}

A recommender system is a function that, given a user $u$, returns a ranked list of items from a catalogue, in this case jobs. The core challenge is predicting which items a user will find relevant before they have interacted with them. Three broad approaches exist in the literature.

\textbf{Collaborative filtering} \cite{glauber2019collaborativefilteringvscontentbased} exploits the collective behaviour of all users: if two users like the same items, they are likely to have similar preferences. Pure collaborative filtering does not require any item or user features --- only the interaction matrix --- but it suffers from the cold-start problem: new users or new items with no interaction history cannot be represented \cite{nadimi2014coldstartproblem}. 

\textbf{Content-based filtering} \cite{glauber2019collaborativefilteringvscontentbased} instead represents each user and item by their features and learns to match them directly. A new user can immediately receive recommendations based on their profile, bypassing the cold-start problem, but the system is limited to recommending similar items to those the user has already interacted with, which can lead to a lack of diversity.

\textbf{Hybrid systems} combine both signals, typically by learning a joint representation that incorporates both interaction history and feature information \cite{li2018hybridrecommendationmethodbased}. The \gls{TT} neural architecture used in this thesis (Chapter~\ref{chap:user_recommendation}) is a state-of-the-art hybrid approach: it operates on user and job features, but the training objective is defined over observed application interactions.

\subsection{Data Representations}
\label{sec:prelim_representations}

Representations are the feature encodings used as inputs to recommendation models. In this thesis we distinguish representation methods (how information is encoded) from model classes (the learning algorithms that consume those features). The main representation methods used are neural embeddings and classical TF‑IDF vectors; both are used throughout the experiments either alone or in combination.

\subsubsection{Neural Embeddings}
\label{sec:prelim_embeddings}

A neural embedding is a learned mapping from a discrete or high-dimensional input to a dense, low-dimensional vector. Embeddings are the core representational tool used throughout this thesis. Two types appear:

\textbf{Learnable embedding layers} are used for categorical features such as city or education level. Each unique category value is assigned a vector of dimension $d_{\text{cat}}$, and these vectors are updated during training by gradient descent. Unlike one-hot encodings, learned embeddings can capture semantic similarity: categories that behave similarly in the training data will converge to nearby vectors.

\textbf{Pre-trained text embeddings} are used to encode natural language fields such as job titles, descriptions and work history. In this thesis, the Qwen3 Embedding model (0.6B parameters) is used to produce continuous vector representations of text. Further discussed in Chapter~\ref{chap:user_recommendation}.

\subsubsection{TF-IDF Representations}
\label{sec:prelim_tfidf}

While neural embeddings capture semantic relationships through learned representations, a simpler and complementary approach is \textbf{Term Frequency–Inverse Document Frequency} (TF-IDF), a classical bag-of-words weighting scheme. Given a corpus of documents, the TF-IDF weight of a term $t$ in document $d$ is:

\begin{equation}
    \text{TF-IDF}(t, d) = \text{TF}(t, d) \cdot \log \frac{|\mathcal{D}|}{|\{d' \in \mathcal{D} : t \in d'\}|}
\end{equation}

where $\text{TF}(t, d)$ is the frequency of term $t$ in document $d$ and $|\mathcal{D}|$ is the total number of documents. Terms that appear often in a specific document but rarely across the corpus receive high weights, making them good discriminators.

Each document is represented as a sparse vector of TF-IDF weights over the full vocabulary. The similarity between two such vectors is then measured using cosine similarity, which captures keyword-level overlap between two texts in a computationally efficient manner. TF-IDF requires no training and is fully interpretable, making it a useful complementary signal when explicit keyword matching is more informative than semantic proximity.

\subsection{Two-Sided Markets and Congestion}
\label{sec:prelim_twosided}

A two-sided market is a platform that serves two distinct groups of participants whose value derives from interacting with each other \cite{mashayekhi2023challengebasedsurveyerecruitmentrecommendation}. The job market is a canonical example: job seekers derive value from being matched to a suitable job, and recruiters or employers derive value from finding suitable candidates. Unlike a one-sided market such as video streaming, where the same item can be consumed by arbitrarily many users simultaneously, the job market operates under \emph{capacity constraints}: each position can be filled by at most one candidate.

This constraint leads to \textbf{market congestion}. Formally, congestion occurs when the recommendation distribution is highly concentrated: a large fraction of users are directed towards a small subset of jobs, resulting in those jobs receiving far more applications than they can process, while other jobs receive too few. Congestion is harmful on both sides: over-exposed jobs create noise and decision fatigue for recruiters, and under-exposed jobs represent missed opportunities for seekers and vacant positions.

Two concrete manifestations of congestion are studied in this thesis:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Job congestion}: a small number of jobs accumulate a disproportionate share of recommended applications. This is closely related to the popularity bias described in Section~\ref{sec:intro:motivation}.
    \item \textbf{Candidate congestion}: a small fraction of candidates 
    receive a disproportionate share of recruiter shortlisting events, while 
    the majority are overlooked entirely. This mirrors job congestion but 
    operates on the recruiter's inbox rather than the job posting's application 
    pool.
\end{itemize}

A key goal of this research is to propose strategies that reduce both forms of congestion while preserving recommendation relevance for individual users.

\section{Machine Learning Background}
\label{sec:prelim_ml_background}

This section reviews the general machine learning techniques employed in this work: the representational tools used to encode user and item features, the lightweight algorithms used for recruiter-side prediction, and the language model prompting strategy used to generate synthetic training labels.

\subsection{Lightweight Machine Learning Models}
\label{sec:prelim_lightweight}


In addition to the neural architecture used for user-side recommendation, this thesis employs several lightweight machine learning algorithms trained on feature vectors composed of engineered signals, TF‑IDF vectors and precomputed neural text embeddings. See Chapter~\ref{chap:recruiter_recommendation} for details. These models include:
\paragraph{Logistic Regression.} A linear model that estimates the 
probability of a binary outcome via a weighted sum of input features. 
It is interpretable and efficient, making it well-suited to the small 
synthetic dataset used for recruiter-side training.

\paragraph{Random Forest.} An ensemble of decision trees that improves 
predictive performance by averaging the outputs of multiple trees trained 
on random subsets of the data and features. It can capture non-linear 
relationships and interactions between features.

\paragraph{XGBoost.} A gradient boosting framework that builds an ensemble 
of weak learners (typically decision trees) in a stage-wise manner, 
optimising a differentiable loss function. It is known for its high 
performance on structured data.

\paragraph{Voting Classifier.} An ensemble method that combines the 
predictions of multiple models (here: Logistic Regression, Random Forest 
and XGBoost) by majority voting, improving robustness and generalisation 
by leveraging the strengths of each.



\subsection{Large Language Models and Prompting}
\label{sec:prelim_llm}
 
A \gls{LLM} is a neural network trained on large amounts of text to predict the probability of the next token in a sequence. Through this objective, \gls{LLM} acquire generalised knowledge about language, facts and reasoning patterns. At inference time, an \gls{LLM} takes a natural language \emph{prompt} as input and generates a continuation --- making it usable as a reasoner without any task-specific retraining.
 
In this thesis, an \gls{LLM} is used not as a recommender system in itself, but as a \textbf{label generator}. Because no ground-truth recruiter feedback exists in the available dataset, an \gls{LLM} is prompted to act as a recruiter and evaluate candidate-job fit using different features and assign a binary label to each pair. The quality of these synthetic labels determines the quality of the recruiter-side model trained on them, making the prompting strategy a critical design choice.
 
\subsubsection{Naive Prompting and Its Limitations}
 
A naive prompt instructs the model to perform a task in a single step: for example, \emph{``Given this candidate profile and job description, is this a good fit? Answer yes or no.''}. In practice, this approach produces inconsistent outputs for structured evaluation tasks. The model may apply different implicit criteria across examples as each prompt is independently generated when making different calls to the model. This leads to inconsistent labels and noise, which consequently degrades the performance of models trained on the labeled data.
\subsubsection{Chain-of-Thought Prompting}
\label{sec:prelim_cot}
 
\gls{CoT} prompting addresses this issue by explicitly instructing the model to reason step by step before arriving at a conclusion \cite{wei2023chainofthoughtpromptingelicitsreasoning}. Rather than asking for a direct answer, the prompt structures the reasoning process which guides the \gls{LLM} to build up its answer from explicit intermediate steps.
 
This structured approach has two practical benefits for label generation. First, it forces the model to be explicit about the criteria it is applying, making inconsistencies visible and correctable through prompt refinement, also known as prompt engineering. Second, it anchors the final label to intermediate reasoning steps, reducing the risk of outputs that are superficially plausible but logically unsupported. The design of the \gls{CoT} prompt used in this thesis is discussed in Chapter~\ref{chap:recruiter_recommendation}.

\section{Evaluation Metrics}
\label{sec:prelim_metrics}

Evaluating models is done by using two families of metrics: classification metrics and ranking metrics. Both families are defined here for reference throughout the experimental chapter.

\subsection{Classification Metrics}
\label{sec:prelim_classification}

Given a binary prediction $\hat{y} \in \{0,1\}$ and ground truth $y \in \{0,1\}$, the standard confusion matrix quantities are true positives (TP), true negatives (TN), false positives (FP) and false negatives (FN).

\textbf{Accuracy} measures the fraction of correctly classified examples:
\begin{equation}
    \text{Accuracy} = \frac{\text{TP} + \text{TN}}{\text{TP} + \text{TN} + \text{FP} + \text{FN}}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Precision} measures what fraction of predicted positives are correct, which is relevant when false recommendations are costly:
\begin{equation}
    \text{Precision} = \frac{\text{TP}}{\text{TP} + \text{FP}}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Recall} measures what fraction of true positives are retrieved, which matters when missing good matches is costly:
\begin{equation}
    \text{Recall} = \frac{\text{TP}}{\text{TP} + \text{FN}}
\end{equation}

\textbf{F1-Score} is the harmonic mean of precision and recall, providing a single summary when both matter:
\begin{equation}
    \text{F1} = 2 \cdot \frac{\text{Precision} \cdot \text{Recall}}{\text{Precision} + \text{Recall}}
\end{equation}

\textbf{AUC-ROC} (Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve) measures the probability that a randomly chosen positive example is scored higher than a randomly chosen negative example. Unlike accuracy, it is threshold-independent and therefore more informative when the positive-negative ratio in the evaluation set differs from the deployment setting (further discussed in Chapter~\ref{chap:experiments}). AUC-ROC is the primary metric for evaluating the user-side recommendation model during training.

\subsection{Ranking Metrics}
\label{sec:prelim_ranking}

Ranking metrics evaluate the quality of a top-$K$ ranked list returned for a user, rather than a single prediction. Let $R_u^K$ denote the top-$K$ recommendations for user $u$ and $\mathcal{J}^{+}_u$ their set of relevant items.

\textbf{Hit@$K$} indicates whether at least one relevant item appears in the top-$K$ list:
\begin{equation}
    \text{Hit@}K = \mathbf{1}\left[ R_u^K \cap \mathcal{J}^{+}_u \neq \emptyset \right]
\end{equation}

\textbf{Recall@$K$} measures the fraction of relevant items that are retrieved within the top-$K$:
\begin{equation}
    \text{Recall@}K = \frac{|R_u^K \cap \mathcal{J}^{+}_u|}{|\mathcal{J}^{+}_u|}
\end{equation}

\textbf{NDCG@$K$} (Normalised Discounted Cumulative Gain) rewards relevant items that appear higher in the ranking, penalising systems that retrieve the right items but rank them poorly. Let $r_i \in \{0,1\}$ be the relevance of the item at position $i$:
\begin{equation}
    \text{DCG@}K = \sum_{i=1}^{K} \frac{r_i}{\log_2(i+1)}, \qquad
    \text{NDCG@}K = \frac{\text{DCG@}K}{\text{IDCG@}K}
\end{equation}
where IDCG@$K$ is the DCG of the ideal (perfectly ranked) list, used for normalisation to $[0,1]$.

An important caveat applies to all ranking metrics: their values depend heavily on the size of the candidate pool against which ranking is performed. Hit@$K$, Recall@$K$ and NDCG@$K$ computed against a small balanced validation set are considerably more optimistic than the same metrics computed against the full job pool at inference. This distinction is discussed further in Chapter~\ref{chap:experiments}, where the implications for interpreting training-time metrics are made explicit. AUC-ROC is less sensitive to this effect and is therefore used as the primary early stopping criterion.

\subsection{Fairness Metric}
\label{sec:prelim_fairness}

Beyond relevance-based metrics, this thesis evaluates recommendation systems
with respect to market fairness — that is, how equitably they distribute
attention across jobs and candidates. The primary fairness metric used is the
\textbf{Gini coefficient}, adapted from income inequality measurement to
quantify concentration in a distribution of counts.

Given a non-negative count vector $\mathbf{x} = (x_1, \ldots, x_n)$, the Gini
coefficient is defined as:
\begin{equation}
    G(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{n + 1 - 2\,\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}
    x_{(i)}\,\big/\!\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i}{n},
    \label{eq:gini}
\end{equation}
where $x_{(1)} \leq x_{(2)} \leq \cdots \leq x_{(n)}$ is the sorted sequence.
The vector $\mathbf{x}$ must include all entities, even those with a count of
zero. A Gini of 0 represents perfect equality (every entity receives the same
count); a Gini of 1 represents complete concentration (one entity receives
everything). In a perfectly fair job market, both the distribution of
applications across jobs and the distribution of shortlisting events across
candidates would have a Gini of 0.
