\chapter{Introduction}
\label{chap:intro}

\section{Context}

Recommender systems are widely used in various domains, such as entertainment \cite{ytrecommender}, e-commerce \cite{recommenderecommerce} and employment \cite{qin2020enhanced}, to help users discover relevant personalized content. The job market represents one of the most consequential settings for such systems: a good match benefits not only the individual job seeker and recruiter, but has measurable implications for productivity, wages and economic growth. Yet, despite decades of progress in recommendation research, applying these systems to the job market remains fundamentally harder than applying them to domains like music or film. The job market is a \emph{two-sided} ecosystem --- both job seekers and recruiters have preferences, constraints and goals --- and satisfying one side without considering the other leads to systemic inefficiencies. This master's dissertation investigates how AI, more specifically recommender systems, can be redesigned to address these inefficiencies.

\section{Motivation}
\label{sec:intro:motivation}

The need to improve job matching goes beyond individual convenience. At a macroeconomic level, inefficient matching degrades productivity across entire labour markets. Both overqualification and underqualification generate real costs: reduced job satisfaction, accelerated burnout and higher turnover \cite{cedefop_skills}. These are not marginal effects --- skills mismatch has been identified as one of the central structural challenges in modern labour markets.

Digitisation was expected to solve this problem by making information more accessible to all parties. In practice, it has introduced new complications: the large number of job listings available online leads to decision fatigue and systematically suboptimal choices for both job seekers and recruiters \cite{nurfadillah2026analysis}. More listings do not mean better matches; they mean a larger search space that neither side can navigate efficiently without algorithmic assistance.

The way current recommendation algorithms provide that assistance, however, introduces a third problem. Standard systems tend to surface the most popular options to the most users, creating a rich-get-richer dynamic known as popularity bias \cite{Klimashevskaia_2024}. In the job market this has concrete consequences: popular jobs attract the majority of all applications, leaving less prominent but equally valid positions under-applied, while highly qualified candidates miss opportunities at companies that never appear in their recommendations \cite{li2023feirquantifyingreducingenvy}. The result is a form of systemic inequity that standard relevance-maximising systems not only fail to correct, but actively perpetuate.

Taken together, these three forces --- macroeconomic mismatch, information overload, and algorithmic inequality of visibility --- motivate a shift towards fairness-aware and congestion-aware recommendation frameworks capable of balancing the needs of both sides of the market.

\section{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:intro:problem}

The job market has a structural property that sets it apart from most recommendation domains: only one candidate can fill any given role, and each candidate can only hold one job. Unlike a streaming service where the same film can be recommended to millions of simultaneous viewers, the job market operates under hard capacity constraints \cite{mashayekhi2023reconreducingcongestionjob}. When digitisation brought recommendation systems to this market, the mismatch between the system's design assumptions and the market's actual structure became a source of new inefficiencies.

The scale of the problem is illustrated by a simple example from \cite{mashayekhi2023reconreducingcongestionjob}: given four job seekers and four job openings, if all four seekers receive the same top-two recommendations, all four will apply to the same two jobs. Only two can be hired. Two jobs remain vacant, two candidates remain unemployed --- not because good matches did not exist, but because the recommendation system created congestion that prevented them from being found. Standard systems fail here in three ways which are connected to each other.

First, they optimise for point-wise relevance without accounting for market capacity. Recommending the same high-relevance job to hundreds of users is locally correct --- the match is genuinely good --- but globally harmful, because it saturates that job with applications while leaving capacity elsewhere unused. Research has shown that explicitly modelling capacity constraints leads to measurably better matching outcomes \cite{mashayekhi2023reconreducingcongestionjob, li2023feirquantifyingreducingenvy}.

Second, existing systems are unidirectional. They are built around one side of the market --- typically the job seeker --- and treat the other side as a static target rather than an active participant with its own preferences and constraints. A recruiter looking for a candidate with a specific set of qualifications is not well-served by a system that has only ever learned what job seekers click on.

Third, the data landscape is deeply asymmetric. Job seeker behaviour --- clicks, views, applications --- is captured at scale by job platforms and is relatively well documented. Recruiter behaviour, by contrast, is largely invisible: decisions about which candidates are reviewed, shortlisted or ultimately hired are typically made internally off-platform, and are further withheld due to privacy concerns. The result is that any system trained on publicly available data — including the dataset used in this work — sees only one side of the hiring conversation. This structural absence of recruiter signal biases learned models towards seeker preferences by default, further compounding the unidirectionality problem described above.

\section{Research Contributions}
\label{sec:intro:contributions}

This thesis proposes a two-sided recommendation framework that addresses all three of the above failures. The framework consists of three components, each of which constitutes a standalone contribution while also serving the larger system.

The first contribution is a \textbf{user-side job recommendation system} based on a two-tower neural network architecture. The model learns dense representations of both users and jobs and computes compatibility via a dot-product similarity, enabling efficient retrieval across large job pools. Importantly, the architecture is designed to handle cold-start users --- an unavoidable reality in any realistic deployment --- by relying on profile features rather than interaction history.

The second contribution is a \textbf{recruiter-side candidate ranking system} that addresses the data sparsity problem directly. Since no ground-truth recruiter feedback is available in existing datasets, synthetic training labels are generated using a \gls{LLM} prompted with \gls{CoT} reasoning to evaluate candidate-job fit across multiple dimensions. A lightweight \gls{XGBoost} model is then trained on these labels using a combination of heuristic and semantic features, yielding an interpretable and computationally efficient ranker that can be deployed alongside the user-side system.

The third contribution is a \textbf{hybrid congestion balancing strategy} that uses the outputs of both preceding systems as inputs. By analysing the recommendation distribution across both sides of the market, the strategy identifies and mitigates over-exposure of popular jobs and under-representation of qualified but overlooked candidates. The result is a more equitable market equilibrium in which recommendation quality is maintained while congestion is measurably reduced.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:prelim_related_work}

The rapid advancement of digital labour platforms has shifted the matching paradigm from simple keyword search engines toward autonomous decision-making systems. As mentioned in Section~\ref{sec:intro:problem}, the job market is a two-sided reciprocal market characterized by rivalrous goods, hard capacity constraints, and heterogeneous preferences on both sides \cite{goda2024bestofbothapproachimprovematch, mashayekhi2023reconreducingcongestionjob}. A successful recommendation in this context is not merely one that elicits a click, but one that leads to a bilateral agreement where both the job seeker and the recruiter find the pairing optimal.

\subsection{Reciprocal Recommendation Paradigms}
The scientific literature classifies \gls{RRS} based on how they integrate the preferences of both parties. 

\subsubsection{Aggregation and Match Prediction}
The traditional approach, known as \gls{PtA}, trains separate models for each direction (user-to-job and job-to-user) and combines their scores using fusion functions like the harmonic mean or product \cite{goda2024bestofbothapproachimprovematch}. To address the error propagation inherent in \gls{PtA}, the recent Best-of-Both (BoB) framework leverages pseudo-match scores—a weighted average of sparse ground-truth labels and dense predictions—to train meta-models that directly output match likelihoods \cite{goda2024bestofbothapproachimprovematch}.

%\subsubsection{Stability and Bandit Learning}
%The theoretical foundation of \gls{RRS} often relies on stable matching theory. Modern research addresses unknown and dynamic preferences through multi-agent bandit learning, such as \gls{MMTS} \cite{Li2024_MMTS}. This allows platforms to learn preferences through interaction while converging toward a stable matching equilibrium that respects participant quotas \cite{Li2024_MMTS, StableMatching2025}.

\subsection{Neural Architectures for Retrieval}
As platforms scale to millions of users, the Two-Tower neural architecture has become the industrial standard for candidate retrieval \cite{Xiong_2025, yuan2024contextgnntwotowerrecommendationsystems}. 

\subsubsection{Two-Tower Enhancements}
Standard Two-Tower models suffer from "Late Interaction," where user and item features do not interact until the final dot product. Recent state-of-the-art enhancements include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Hierarchical Interaction-Enhanced Two-Tower (HIT):} Augments the paradigm with multi-head representers to project embeddings into multi-faceted interest dimensions \cite{yang2025hitmodelhierarchicalinteractionenhanced}.
    \item \textbf{biLorentzFM:} Utilizes hyperbolic embeddings to capture the hierarchical nature of job markets (e.g., senior roles requiring specific junior experiences) \cite{biLorentzFM2025}.
    \item \textbf{ContextGNN:} Fuses pair-wise representations for local interaction graphs with global Two-Tower representations for exploratory items \cite{yuan2024contextgnntwotowerrecommendationsystems}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Market Congestion and Distributional Equity}
Market congestion occurs when recommendation policies concentrate attention on a tiny subset of popular job postings, leading to "orphan jobs" that receive zero applications \cite{01KAK08E4756SJAF6RCBJVGWXB, mashayekhi2023challengebasedsurveyerecruitmentrecommendation}. This concentration degrades the welfare of all job seekers and creates information overload and vacancy gaps for employers.

%\subsubsection{Measuring Inequality}
%To quantify this phenomenon, the research community has adopted the \textit{Gini coefficient} from economics to measure concentration in recommendation distributions \cite{Do2022, GiniFairness2021}. A Gini coefficient near 1 indicates extreme concentration, prompting the need for "market-maker" interventions that act as coordinates for the labor pool \cite{JoLA2025}.

\subsubsection{Algorithmic Mitigation}
Advanced strategies for mitigation include \textit{in-processing} methods 
that modify the recommendation objective directly. The \textit{ReCon} model 
integrates Optimal Transport (OT) theory to redistribute application flow 
away from congested jobs \cite{mashayekhi2023reconreducingcongestionjob}. 
\textit{JoLA} (Job Landscape Aware) introduces differentiable market share 
approximations to specifically reduce the number of orphan job ads 
\cite{01KAK08E4756SJAF6RCBJVGWXB}. \textit{FEIR} takes a complementary 
fairness perspective, quantifying and reducing envy and inferiority in 
recommendations of limited resources such as job positions 
\cite{li2023feirquantifyingreducingenvy}.

\subsection{Reasoning-Based Recruiter Suitability Modeling}
A persistent challenge is data asymmetry: seeker behavioural signals are captured at scale, but recruiter decision signals (shortlisting, hiring) are often invisible or off-platform \cite{mashayekhi2023challengebasedsurveyerecruitmentrecommendation}.

\subsubsection{LLM-Based Synthetic Feedback}
To overcome data scarcity, state-of-the-art research leverages Large Language Models (LLMs) to generate synthetic labels for candidate suitability \cite{gan2024applicationllmagentsrecruitment}. These models possess high \textit{Algorithmic Fidelity}, enabling them to simulate expert professional judgments. Dominant methodologies involve \gls{CoT} prompting to extract explicit criteria (Must-Haves, Deal-Breakers) from job descriptions and reason through candidate fit \cite{wei2023chainofthoughtpromptingelicitsreasoning}.
