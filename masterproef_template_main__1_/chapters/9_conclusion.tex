\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{chap:conclusion}

This thesis set out to address a structural inefficiency in modern digital
labour markets. Basic recommender systems concentrate attention on a small 
subset of popular job postings, creating a feedback loop that leaves the 
majority of positions under-applied and a large fraction of qualified 
candidates overlooked. Three contributions were developed and evaluated to 
tackle this problem from both sides of the market.

The first contribution is a user-side job recommender based on a \gls{TT}
neural architecture. The model learns dense representations of job seekers and
job postings and measures their compatibility via dot-product similarity. A 
stratified negative sampling strategy, mixing geographically distant, 
same-state, and same-city negatives in a 2:1:1 ratio, was shown to be 
essential for teaching the model to discriminate on career content rather than 
coarse geographic proximity. An ablation study established that including 
location features and omitting L2 normalisation yields the strongest 
performance, with an \gls{AUC} of 0.9242 on the validation set.

The second contribution is a recruiter-side suitability model. Because no 
ground-truth labels are available for recruiter decisions, a large language 
model was prompted with structured \gls{CoT} reasoning to generate synthetic 
candidate-fit labels. A lightweight XGBoost classifier was then trained on a 
feature set combining heuristic and semantic signals. A two-stage model 
selection process identified XGBoost trained on combined negatives (equal 
parts LLM-hard and synthetic random) as the most robust choice for the dual 
purpose it serves in the pipeline, achieving an AUC of 0.755.

The third contribution is a congestion-aware two-sided job matching framework
that integrates both models into a sequential two-phase pipeline. Phase~1 
generates job recommendations using a bilateral score combining \gls{TT} 
relevance and recruiter suitability, augmented by a job-side market-maker 
penalty that progressively suppresses over-applied jobs and boosts 
under-applied ones. Phase~2 re-ranks each job's applicant inbox using the 
recruiter model, augmented by a candidate-side penalty that redistributes 
shortlisting attention toward overlooked candidates. Among the three penalty 
forms evaluated, the sigmoid performed best on the job side, while all three 
converged on the candidate side due to the narrower congestion range.

The experimental evaluation on the CareerBuilder dataset shows that an 
uncorrected \gls{TT} recommender actively worsens congestion relative to 
organic behaviour which confirms that unmodified systems are not neutral. The 
complete hybrid pipeline reverses this: the job-side Gini falls by 26\% and 
the fraction of starved jobs drops by 27 percentage points, while the 
candidate-side Gini falls by 37\% and starved candidates drop from 32.1\% to 
just 3.4\%. These gains come at a modest cost of 3 percentage points in 
\gls{TT} probability, while the average recruiter suitability remains high 
at 0.8106. 

These results demonstrate that the congestion problem in job markets is not 
simply a side effect of popularity bias, but is actively reinforced by 
relevance-optimised recommenders. Addressing it requires coordinating both 
sides of the market: steering users toward jobs where they are genuinely 
competitive, and ensuring recruiters surface candidates who have not already 
been claimed by competing inboxes. The framework developed in this thesis 
shows that this coordination is achievable without sacrificing match quality, 
and points toward a more equitable model of algorithmic hiring assistance.
