\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{chap:conclusion}

This thesis set out to address a structural inefficiency in modern digital
labour markets: the tendency of recommender systems to concentrate candidate
attention on a small subset of popular job postings, leaving the majority of
positions under-applied and a large fraction of qualified candidates
overlooked. Three interlocking contributions were developed and evaluated to
tackle this problem from both sides of the market.

The first contribution is a user-side job recommender based on a Two-Tower
neural architecture. The model learns dense representations of job seekers and
job postings and measures their compatibility via dot-product similarity,
enabling efficient retrieval across large catalogues. A stratified negative
sampling strategy --- mixing geographically distant, same-state, and same-city
negatives in a 2:1:1 ratio --- was shown to be essential for teaching the
model to discriminate on career content rather than on coarse geographic
proximity alone. An ablation study established that including location features
and omitting L2 normalisation on the output embeddings yields the strongest
performance, with an AUC-ROC of 0.9242 on the validation set. Qualitative
inspection at inference time confirmed that the model produces geographically
coherent and semantically tight recommendations for users with clear career
signals, and gracefully falls back to location- and profile-based
recommendations for cold-start users with no recorded interaction history ---
a realistic and unavoidable scenario in any production deployment.

The second contribution is a recruiter-side suitability model that
directly addresses the data asymmetry inherent in publicly available job
matching datasets: while job-seeker behaviour is recorded at scale, recruiter
decisions are largely off-platform and invisible. To bridge this gap, a large
language model (Qwen3.5, 35B parameters) was prompted with structured
Chain-of-Thought reasoning to generate synthetic candidate-fit labels,
 producing a dataset of 5,896 labelled $(u,j)$ pairs across 1,511 unique
jobs. A lightweight XGBoost classifier was then trained on a feature set
combining heuristic signals (experience delta, TF-IDF keyword overlap, degree
alignment) with semantic signals (Qwen3 embedding interaction matrix, MiniLM
full-profile similarity). Feature importance analysis revealed that the
PCA-compressed embedding interaction matrix dominates model decisions,
accounting for over 85\% of importance, confirming that element-wise
interactions between candidate and job embeddings capture the bulk of the
discriminative signal. A two-stage model selection process --- first choosing
the classifier family, then the negative construction strategy --- identified
XGBoost trained on combined negatives (equal parts LLM-hard and synthetic
random) as the best configuration, achieving AUC 0.755 on a mixed test set
while generalising to both the within-applicant-pool ranking scenario and the
broader candidate-scoring context required during congestion balancing.

The third contribution is a dual-sided hybrid congestion balancing framework
that integrates both models into a sequential two-phase pipeline. Phase~1
generates job recommendations for users using a bilateral combined score that
weights the Two-Tower relevance signal and the recruiter suitability score
equally, optionally augmented by a job-side market-maker penalty that
progressively suppresses over-applied jobs and boosts under-applied ones.
Phase~2 re-ranks each job's applicant inbox using the recruiter model,
optionally augmented by a candidate-side market-maker penalty that
redistributes shortlisting attention away from candidates who have already been
surfaced frequently. Three functional forms for the penalty --- linear,
quadratic, and sigmoid --- were evaluated; the sigmoid form performed best on
the job side due to its smooth, bounded redistribution across a wide congestion
range, while all three forms converged on nearly identical outcomes on the
candidate side, a structural consequence of the narrower congestion range on
that side of the market.

The experimental evaluation was conducted on the CareerBuilder dataset using a
rigorous temporal window split, with all model selection performed on
Windows~1--6 and the final evaluation on the held-out Window~7. The results
demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed framework at multiple levels. An
uncorrected Two-Tower recommender was shown to worsen congestion relative to
organic user behaviour, with the job-side Gini rising from 0.8575 to 0.9625
and the starved job fraction increasing from 80.9\% to 90.8\%. This confirms
that relevance-optimising systems are not neutral: they actively amplify the
feedback loops that concentrate traffic. The bilateral scoring component alone
--- combining both model outputs without any explicit penalty --- already
produces a meaningful improvement, reducing the job-side Gini to 0.9182 while
simultaneously raising the average recruiter suitability probability from
0.4976 to 0.8405, indicating that users are being steered toward jobs where
they are genuinely competitive candidates rather than merely interested ones.
The full dual-sided hybrid strategy (bilateral scoring plus sigmoid penalties
on both sides) achieves the strongest results across all metrics: the job-side
Gini coefficient falls from 0.9182 to 0.7130 and the fraction of starved jobs
drops from 85.6\% to 63.3\%, while the candidate-side Gini falls from 0.6326
to 0.4967 and the fraction of starved candidates drops from 32.1\% to just
3.4\%. These improvements come at a match quality cost of 3.6\% in average
Two-Tower probability relative to the bilateral baseline --- a modest price for
a substantially more equitable market-level distribution, and one that leaves
the large recruiter suitability gain from bilateral scoring (from 0.4976 to
0.8106) firmly intact.

Taken together, these results demonstrate that coordinating user-side
recommendation and recruiter-side ranking is an effective lever for congestion
mitigation in large-scale labour platforms. The two-sided nature of the job
market is not merely a modelling complication --- it is an opportunity. By
treating both job seekers and recruiters as active participants with their own
preferences, and by explicitly balancing the distribution of attention across
both sides, it is possible to move the market toward an equilibrium that is
fairer for candidates, more manageable for recruiters, and more efficient
overall.
