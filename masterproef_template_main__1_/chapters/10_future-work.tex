\chapter*{Future Work}
\chaptermark{Future Work}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Future Work}  
\label{ch:future-work}

While the proposed framework produces strong quantitative results and qualitatively sensible behaviour,
several structural limitations constrain the current design. These are discussed per component below,
together with the most natural directions for future work.

\section{User-Side Recommender}

The Two-Tower model operates on implicit feedback: application events are treated as binary
positive signals, equating \emph{applied to} with \emph{good fit}. This assumption is a known
limitation of interaction-log-based training~\cite{yin2026correctweightsimpleeffective}. A
user may apply speculatively to positions for which they are underqualified, or conversely
fail to apply to a highly relevant job due to limited visibility. This label noise is
structural rather than incidental, and any model trained on application logs inherits it. A
natural direction for future work would be to incorporate downstream hiring signals ---
interview invitations, shortlisting decisions, or hiring outcomes --- to replace the current
binary scheme with graded relevance labels, aligning the training objective more closely with
the platform's actual goal of facilitating successful matches.

A second direction concerns the training objective itself. The model is optimised with
Binary Cross-Entropy loss, which treats each example independently and does not directly
optimise for ranking quality. The deployment task, however, is a ranking problem: what
matters is whether the most relevant jobs appear at the top of the list, not whether each
pair is classified correctly in isolation. Replacing BCE with a ranking-aware objective such
as Bayesian Personalised Ranking~\cite{rendle2012bprbayesianpersonalizedranking} would
align training more directly with the evaluation metrics and could improve top-$K$
performance without any architectural change.

The negative sampling strategy offers a third avenue for improvement. Negatives are
constructed once at dataset creation time using a fixed mixture of geographically proximal
and distant jobs, and this set remains unchanged throughout training. Early in training, when
the model is still a poor discriminator, these negatives are genuinely challenging. As the
model improves, however, the same examples become trivially easy to reject, providing
diminishing learning signal. A dynamic strategy --- where negatives are refreshed during
training by selecting jobs the model currently scores as nearly relevant but that are
in fact incorrect --- would maintain a consistent level of difficulty throughout and force the
model to refine its representations beyond what a fixed negative set allows \cite{10.1145/2484028.2484126}.

At the architectural level, the two towers process their respective inputs in complete
isolation: the user tower reads the user profile and produces a single vector; the job tower
does the same for the job. The only moment the two representations interact is the final dot
product, which reduces compatibility to a single geometric similarity score. This means the
model cannot capture conditional relationships between features --- for instance, that
geographic proximity matters more for junior roles than for senior ones, or that a specific
skill in the user profile is only relevant given a particular job category. A recruiter
reading a CV alongside a job description naturally reasons across both simultaneously; the
two-tower design structurally prevents this. Introducing cross-interactions between the two
sides during encoding, as proposed by~\cite{su2023twotowermatchinglearningsparse}, could capture these
dependencies and improve matching precision, though at the cost of the caching efficiency
that makes the current design practical at scale.

Finally, the popularity bias present in the historical application log is reflected in the
training data: jobs that attracted many applications are well-represented in positive pairs
and tend to produce large, well-separated embeddings, while rarely-applied-to jobs are
underrepresented. The congestion correction described in
Chapter~\ref{chap:congestion_balancing} addresses this post-hoc via score adjustments at
inference time, but the underlying embedding space remains skewed toward popular jobs. A
more principled direction for future work would be to incorporate the congestion objective
directly into the training procedure, so that the model learns to produce fairer
representations from the start rather than correcting for bias downstream. The work of
\cite{mashayekhi2023reconreducingcongestionjob} demonstrates this in-processing paradigm by combining an optimal transport component with a
job recommendation model in a joint optimisation problem, showing that congestion can be
reduced without sacrificing recommendation quality.

\section{Recruiter-Side Recommender}

The most fundamental limitation of the recruiter-side component is that the model learns to
replicate the judgments of a large language model, not the decisions of actual human
recruiters. LLM-generated labels are a proxy for ground truth: the labeler may carry biases
inherited from its pretraining data, and despite the structured Chain-of-Thought prompt,
may apply inconsistent implicit thresholds across independent calls. This is a documented
concern in LLM-as-judge settings more broadly~\cite{iso2025llmbias}, with research
specifically in recruitment contexts showing that implicit biases around educational
background remain significant even in recent models~\cite{iso2025llmbias}. Any downstream
behaviour of the recruiter-side model inherits these biases. Access to real recruiter
feedback --- shortlisting decisions, interview invitations, or hiring outcomes --- would
remove this layer of indirection entirely and allow the model to learn from ground truth
rather than a simulation of it.

A second limitation concerns the small size of the labelled dataset. With fewer than 6,000
candidate--job pairs, the XGBoost classifier is trained on a narrow slice of the full
diversity of candidate--job combinations present in the CareerBuilder dataset. Performance on
unusual or underrepresented job categories is difficult to assess, and the model may not
generalise to patterns outside what it has seen. The bottleneck here is the computational
cost of LLM-based labelling at scale. Future work could address this through faster
inference, batched prompt optimisation, or by distilling the LLM's judgments into a smaller,
faster labelling model that can be run over a substantially larger sample.

A third limitation is that several heuristic features rely on brittle regex extraction. Both
the experience delta $\delta_{\text{exp}}$ and the degree match feature are derived by
parsing free-text job postings with hand-crafted patterns. When a posting does not follow a
standard format, the regex fails silently: the experience delta defaults to zero and degree
requirements go undetected. This means the features are informative only when the posting is
well-structured, and they carry no signal otherwise. A future direction would be to replace
regex extraction with a structured information extraction model fine-tuned on job postings,
which would be more robust to formatting variation and could recover requirements from a
broader range of posting styles.

Fourth, the four-level LLM fit judgments --- \textit{Very Good Fit}, \textit{Good Fit},
\textit{Poor Fit}, \textit{Very Poor Fit} --- are collapsed to binary labels before
training. This discards a meaningful distinction: a borderline \textit{Good Fit} and a
\textit{Very Good Fit} are treated identically as positives, and the model has no signal to
distinguish between them. Training directly on the ordinal fit levels using a ranking or
ordinal regression objective would preserve this gradient and give the model a richer
supervisory signal, potentially producing better-calibrated suitability scores at inference
time.

Finally, the semantic similarity features used by the recruiter-side model are derived from
Qwen3 embeddings that were optimised for application prediction in the user-side two-tower
system --- not for recruiter-side suitability assessment. These are different tasks: the
former captures what a job seeker is likely to apply to, while the latter captures whether a
recruiter would consider a candidate qualified. Retraining or fine-tuning the text encoder
specifically on recruiter-labelled pairs, rather than borrowing embeddings from the
application-prediction task, could better align the semantic representation with the
suitability notion the recruiter-side model is trying to learn.
