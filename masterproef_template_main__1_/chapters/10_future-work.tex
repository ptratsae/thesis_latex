\chapter{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{ch:future-work}

While the proposed framework produces strong quantitative results and qualitatively sensible behaviour,
several structural limitations constrain the current design. These are discussed per component below,
together with the most natural directions for future work.

\section{User-Side Recommender}

The Two-Tower model is a strong baseline, but every aspect of its design --- the feature set, the
network architecture, the training objective, and the hyperparameters --- represents a starting point
rather than an optimised endpoint. More expressive architectures such as those with cross-attention or
hierarchical interaction layers could capture richer user--job relationships. The feature engineering
pipeline could be extended with additional signals, such as industry sector, contract type, or salary
range, and the negative sampling strategy could be made adaptive rather than fixed. In short, the
model provides a solid foundation, but substantial headroom for improvement remains across all of its
components.

\section{Recruiter-Side Recommender}

The recruiter-side component carries three limitations that are more fundamental in nature.

\paragraph{LLM labels as a proxy for ground truth.}
The model is trained to replicate the judgments of a large language model acting as a synthetic
recruiter, not the decisions of actual human recruiters. LLM-generated labels are a proxy: they may
reflect biases present in the model's training data, apply inconsistent implicit standards despite
the structured Chain-of-Thought prompt, and fail to capture the contextual and interpersonal factors
that influence real hiring decisions. Any downstream behaviour of the recruiter-side model therefore
inherits these limitations. Access to real recruiter feedback --- shortlisting decisions, interview
invitations, or hiring outcomes --- would be a transformative improvement and would allow the model
to learn from ground truth rather than a simulation of it.

\paragraph{Small training set.}
The labelled dataset contains only 5{,}896 candidate--job pairs, a direct consequence of the
computational cost of LLM-based labelling at scale. This limits the model's capacity and
generalisability: the XGBoost classifier may not capture the full diversity of candidate--job
combinations present in the broader CareerBuilder dataset, and performance on unusual or
underrepresented job categories is difficult to assess. Scaling up the labelling pipeline ---
through faster inference, batch optimisation, or distillation into a smaller labelling model ---
would allow a substantially larger and more representative training set to be constructed.

\paragraph{Prompt and feature engineering.}
The Chain-of-Thought prompt used to generate labels was developed iteratively but remains a
hand-crafted artefact. A more systematic prompt optimisation process, or the use of few-shot
examples drawn from validated recruiter judgments, could improve label quality and consistency.
Similarly, the feature engineering pipeline for the recruiter-side model, while carefully motivated,
does not exhaust the space of useful signals. Richer semantic representations, structured extraction
of job requirements, or features derived from the candidate's full application history rather than
only their most recent titles could all improve the model's discriminative power.

\section{Congestion Balancing Framework}

\paragraph{Sequential rather than joint optimisation.}
The hybrid framework operates as a two-phase sequential pipeline: Phase~1 determines which jobs
users apply to, and Phase~2 re-ranks candidates within the resulting inboxes. The two phases are
independent --- they do not communicate during execution and are not jointly optimised. As a
consequence, any distributional biases introduced by Phase~1 are silently inherited by Phase~2
with no corrective mechanism. A joint optimisation formulation --- for example, one that explicitly
models the downstream shortlisting distribution when generating recommendations in Phase~1 --- could
reduce this coupling and potentially achieve better market equilibrium with smaller individual
quality costs.

\paragraph{Dilution of the job-side penalty.}
The job congestion penalty in Phase~1 acts only on the Two-Tower component of the combined score,
which carries weight $\alpha = 0.5$. The recruiter probability term is congestion-unaware and pulls
the combined score in the opposite direction, effectively halving the penalty's redistribution power
relative to the standalone job-side experiment. The weighting parameter $\alpha$ therefore controls
a fundamental trade-off between match quality and congestion reduction, and its optimal value is
likely task- and dataset-dependent. Future work could explore adaptive or learned values of $\alpha$,
or redesign the scoring function so that the congestion correction acts on the combined score directly
rather than on one of its components in isolation.

\section{Evaluation}

The entire framework is evaluated through counterfactual simulation on historical application logs
rather than in a live deployment. This means that behavioural feedback loops --- users adjusting
their application behaviour in response to changed recommendations, or recruiters updating their
screening criteria as candidate distributions shift --- are not captured. Real-world deployment would
almost certainly produce effects not visible in the simulation, and a live A/B test or field
experiment would be necessary to validate the framework's impact on actual labour market outcomes.
